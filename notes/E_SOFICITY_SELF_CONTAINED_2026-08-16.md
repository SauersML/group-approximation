## Theorem

Let \(E\) be the eight-generator, forty-one-relator group of `def:E` in
`non_mf_groups_exist.tex`:

\[
E=\left\langle v_1,v_2,v_3,x,y,z,t,c\ \middle|\
\begin{array}{l}
\text{the twenty base relations},\\
 t v_i t^{-1}=v_i^2\quad(1\le i\le 3),\\
[t,x]=[t,y]=[t,z]=1,\\
c^2=1,\\
[c,g]=1\quad(g\in\{v_1,v_2,v_3,x,y,z\}),\\
[w,g]=1\quad(g\in\{v_1,v_2,v_3,x,y,z,t,c\})
\end{array}
\right\rangle,
\]

where

\[
w=[tct^{-1},\,v_1(tct^{-1})v_1^{-1}].
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{E\text{ is sofic}.}
\]

Consequently, combining this with the already established non-MF theorem for
this same literal presentation, \(E\) is an explicit finitely presented
sofic (hence hyperlinear) group which is not MF. In particular its sofic
radical is trivial, while its MF radical is nontrivial.

An earlier manuscript snapshot left soficity of \(E\) open.  The argument
below resolves that question positively and is now assembled unconditionally
in `GroupApproximation/Sofic/LiteralSoficAssembly.lean`.

## 1. The base group and the doubling endomorphism

Write \(\mathcal B\) for the group on
\(v_1,v_2,v_3,x,y,z\) with the twenty base relators displayed in the
manuscript. The repository now proves, without a literature axiom, that the
affine evaluation is an isomorphism

\[
\mathcal B\cong B:=\mathbb Z^3\rtimes \operatorname{SL}_3(\mathbb Z).
\tag{1.1}
\]

This is the theorem
`LiteralBaseCompleteness.baseAffineEquiv` in
`GroupApproximation/Monsters/LiteralBaseCompleteness.lean`.
Its proof separates the presented group into its translation and rotation
parts, proves injectivity of the rotation matrix model by the in-repository
P13 descent, writes every translation as
\(v_1^a v_2^b v_3^c\), and finally observes that the affine matrix of an
element can be the identity only when both its rotation part and all three
translation exponents are trivial.

Henceforth we identify \(\mathcal B\) with \(B\). Define

\[
\alpha:B\to B,\qquad \alpha(u,A)=(2u,A).
\tag{1.2}
\]

This is injective. Its image is

\[
\alpha(B)=2\mathbb Z^3\rtimes \operatorname{SL}_3(\mathbb Z),
\]

of index \(8\) in \(B\).

The element \(v_1\) corresponds to \((e_1,I)\), and
\(v_1\notin\alpha(B)\), since \(e_1\notin 2\mathbb Z^3\).

## 2. The ascending HNN part

Set

\[
T:=\mathbb Z[1/2]^3\rtimes \operatorname{SL}_3(\mathbb Z).
\tag{2.1}
\]

Let \(\tau\) act on \(T\) by

\[
\tau(u,A)\tau^{-1}=(2u,A).
\tag{2.2}
\]

Since multiplication by \(2\) is an automorphism of
\(\mathbb Z[1/2]^3\), this is an automorphism of \(T\). Put

\[
V:=T\rtimes\langle\tau\rangle\cong T\rtimes\mathbb Z.
\tag{2.3}
\]

The subgroup generated by \(B\) and \(\tau\) is all of \(V\), because

\[
(2^{-n}u,I)=\tau^{-n}(u,I)\tau^n.
\]

Moreover

\[
\tau b\tau^{-1}=\alpha(b)\qquad(b\in B).
\]

Thus the presentation on the first seven generators
\(v_1,v_2,v_3,x,y,z,t\), with the twenty base relations and the six
stable-letter relations, is the ascending HNN extension of \(B\) along
\(\alpha\), and by the standard normal-form/Britton theorem it is isomorphic
to \(V\), with \(t\) corresponding to \(\tau\).

For \(n\ge 0\), define the increasing telescope levels

\[
\Gamma_n:=\tau^{-n}B\tau^n
       =2^{-n}\mathbb Z^3\rtimes\operatorname{SL}_3(\mathbb Z).
\tag{2.4}
\]

Then

\[
T=\bigcup_{n\ge0}\Gamma_n.
\tag{2.5}
\]

### Lemma 2.1. Each \(\Gamma_n\) is finitely generated and residually finite.

**Proof.** Scaling the translation coordinate by \(2^n\) identifies
\(\Gamma_n\) with \(B\), so finite generation is immediate.

Residual finiteness can be seen directly by congruence quotients. If
\((u,A)\ne(0,I)\) in \(B\), choose an integer \(m\ge2\) such that either a
nonzero coordinate of \(u\) stays nonzero modulo \(m\), or a nonzero entry of
\(A-I\) stays nonzero modulo \(m\). Reduction modulo \(m\) gives a
homomorphism

\[
B\longrightarrow (\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z)^3
   \rtimes \operatorname{SL}_3(\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z)
\]

to a finite group which does not kill \((u,A)\). Hence \(B\), and therefore
every \(\Gamma_n\), is residually finite. \(\square\)

## 3. Removing the centrality relators: a free lamp kernel

Let \(\widehat E\) be obtained from the presentation of \(E\) by deleting
only the eight relations asserting that \(w\) is central. Thus
\(\widehat E\) is generated by \(V\) and one extra involution \(c\), with
\(c\) commuting with \(B\).

Equivalently,

\[
\widehat E\cong V *_B (B\times C_2),
\tag{3.1}
\]

where the generator of the \(C_2\)-factor is \(c\).

There is an evident retraction

\[
\pi:\widehat E\to V,
\qquad \pi|_V=\operatorname{id},\quad \pi(c)=1.
\tag{3.2}
\]

Let

\[
N:=\ker\pi.
\]

Put

\[
X:=V/B.
\tag{3.3}
\]

For \(g\in V\), set

\[
c_{gB}:=gcg^{-1}.
\tag{3.4}
\]

This is well defined because \(c\) centralizes \(B\).

### Lemma 3.1. The kernel \(N\) is the free product of one involution for each site:

\[
N\cong *_{\xi\in X}\langle c_\xi\mid c_\xi^2=1\rangle
   \cong *_{X} C_2,
\tag{3.5}
\]

and \(V\) acts by permuting the factors according to its natural action on
\(X\).

**Proof.** Use the Bass--Serre tree of the amalgam (3.1). The vertex
stabilizers are conjugates of \(V\) and \(B\times C_2\), while edge
stabilizers are conjugates of \(B\). Since \(N\cap V=1\), the intersection of
\(N\) with a conjugate of a \(V\)-vertex stabilizer is trivial. Its
intersection with a conjugate of \(B\times C_2\) is the corresponding
conjugate of \(\langle c\rangle\cong C_2\), and its intersection with every
edge stabilizer is trivial. The quotient graph for the \(N\)-action is a
star, with one leaf for every coset in \(V/B\). The graph of groups therefore
has trivial edge groups and leaf vertex groups \(C_2\), so its fundamental
group is precisely the free product in (3.5). Conjugation by \(V\) sends
\(c_{gB}\) to \(c_{vgB}\), giving the asserted action. \(\square\)

Let

\[
o:=B\in X,
\qquad a:=\tau o,
\qquad b:=v_1\tau o.
\tag{3.6}
\]

Then in \(N\),

\[
tct^{-1}=c_a,
\qquad
v_1(tct^{-1})v_1^{-1}=c_b,
\]

and hence

\[
w=[c_a,c_b].
\tag{3.7}
\]

The two sites are distinct: \(a=b\) would say
\(v_1\in\tau B\tau^{-1}=\alpha(B)\), contrary to
\(v_1\notin\alpha(B)\).

## 4. Reimposing centrality and the orbital graph

Return to \(E\). Since \(w\in N\), killing the commutators of \(w\) with the
eight generators does not disturb the retraction to \(V\). Thus

\[
E=N_E\rtimes V,
\qquad N_E:=\ker(E\to V).
\tag{4.1}
\]

The quotient from \(N\) to \(N_E\) can be described exactly.

Let \(\zeta\) denote the image of \(w\). Centrality of \(w\) implies both

\[
\zeta\in Z(N_E)
\tag{4.2}
\]

and, for every \(g\in V\),

\[
[c_{ga},c_{gb}]=\zeta.
\tag{4.3}
\]

Conversely, these relations generate precisely the effect on \(N\) of the
centrality relators: centrality against \(N\) says (4.2), while centrality
against \(V\) identifies every \(V\)-conjugate of \([c_a,c_b]\) with the
same element \(\zeta\).

Because \(c_a\) and \(c_b\) are involutions, conjugation by \(c_a\) sends
\([c_a,c_b]\) to its inverse. Since \(\zeta\) is central, it is fixed by that
conjugation. Therefore

\[
\zeta=\zeta^{-1},\qquad \zeta^2=1.
\tag{4.4}
\]

Define a graph \(\mathcal G\) on \(X\) by declaring \(\{\xi,\eta\}\) to be
an edge exactly when it is a \(V\)-translate of \(\{a,b\}\). Then

\[
N_E\cong
\left\langle \zeta,(c_\xi)_{\xi\in X}\ \middle|\
\begin{array}{l}
\zeta^2=c_\xi^2=1,\\
\zeta\text{ central},\\
[c_\xi,c_\eta]=\zeta\quad\text{for }\{\xi,\eta\}\in E(\mathcal G)
\end{array}
\right\rangle.
\tag{4.5}
\]

So the remaining problem is to compute \(\mathcal G\).

## 5. Exact computation of the site set and its blocks

Every element of \(V=T\rtimes\langle\tau\rangle\) is uniquely of the form
\(s\tau^n\), with \(s=(u,A)\in T\) and \(n\in\mathbb Z\). Therefore

\[
X=V/B
\cong
\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb Z}
\mathbb Z[1/2]^3/2^n\mathbb Z^3.
\tag{5.1}
\]

The coset \(s\tau^nB\) corresponds to

\[
(n,[u]_{2^n\mathbb Z^3}).
\]

Indeed,

\[
\tau^nB\tau^{-n}=2^n\mathbb Z^3\rtimes\operatorname{SL}_3(\mathbb Z),
\]

and the linear part disappears in the quotient because it lies in the
stabilizer.

If \(s=(u_s,A_s)\in T\), then

\[
(s\tau^m)\cdot(n,[u])
 =\bigl(n+m,[u_s+A_s2^m u]\bigr).
\tag{5.2}
\]

In these coordinates

\[
o=(0,[0]),\qquad a=(1,[0]),\qquad b=(1,[e_1]).
\tag{5.3}
\]

### Lemma 5.1. The orbital graph \(\mathcal G\) is a disjoint union of complete graphs \(K_8\).

More precisely, two sites \((n,[u])\) and \((n',[u'])\) are adjacent if and
only if

1. \(n=n'\);
2. \(u'-u\in 2^{n-1}\mathbb Z^3\pmod{2^n\mathbb Z^3}\); and
3. \(u'\ne u\pmod{2^n\mathbb Z^3}\).

Thus the blocks at level \(n\) are the cosets of

\[
2^{n-1}\mathbb Z^3/2^n\mathbb Z^3\cong(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^3,
\tag{5.4}
\]

and every block has exactly eight sites.

**Proof.** Apply (5.2) to the ordered marked pair \((a,b)\). For
\(g=s\tau^m\), with \(s=(u_s,A_s)\), the two images are at level
\(n=m+1\), and their translation coordinates differ by

\[
2^mA_se_1=2^{n-1}A_se_1.
\tag{5.5}
\]

Modulo \(2^n\mathbb Z^3\), this depends only on
\(A_se_1\pmod{2\mathbb Z^3}\).

The reduction map

\[
\operatorname{SL}_3(\mathbb Z)\to\operatorname{SL}_3(\mathbb F_2)
\]

is onto: \(\operatorname{SL}_3(\mathbb F_2)\) is generated by elementary
matrices, and these lift to integer elementary matrices. Since
\(\mathbb F_2^\times=\{1\}\),
\(\operatorname{SL}_3(\mathbb F_2)=\operatorname{GL}_3(\mathbb F_2)\), which
acts transitively on the seven nonzero vectors of \(\mathbb F_2^3\).
Therefore, as \(A_s\) varies, (5.5) runs through exactly the seven nonzero
elements of

\[
2^{n-1}\mathbb Z^3/2^n\mathbb Z^3.
\]

The translation part \(u_s\) is arbitrary in \(\mathbb Z[1/2]^3\), so every
site has precisely those seven neighbors. Hence the eight sites in any coset
of (5.4) are pairwise adjacent, and no edge joins two different such cosets
or two different levels. \(\square\)

Let \(I\) denote the set of these blocks. The action of \(V\) on sites
permutes the blocks.

### Lemma 5.2. Every telescope level \(\Gamma_n\) has finite orbits on the block set \(I\).

**Proof.** A block at level \(m\) is determined by a class

\[
[u]\in \mathbb Z[1/2]^3/2^{m-1}\mathbb Z^3.
\tag{5.6}
\]

Choose \(k\) large enough that
\(u\in2^{-k}\mathbb Z^3\) and
\(2^{m-1}\mathbb Z^3\subseteq 2^{-k}\mathbb Z^3\). An element of
\(\Gamma_n\) has the form \((a,A)\) with
\(a\in2^{-n}\mathbb Z^3\) and
\(A\in\operatorname{SL}_3(\mathbb Z)\). Hence the orbit of \([u]\) is
contained in

\[
2^{-j}\mathbb Z^3/2^{m-1}\mathbb Z^3,
\qquad j:=\max\{n,k\},
\tag{5.7}
\]

which is finite. \(\square\)

## 6. The lamp kernel is a central amalgam of finite Clifford blocks

For a block \(i\in I\), let \(P_i\) be the subgroup of \(N_E\) generated by
\(\zeta\) and the eight lamps \(c_\xi\) with \(\xi\in i\). By Lemma 5.1,

\[
P_i=\left\langle \zeta,c_1,\dots,c_8\ \middle|\
\begin{array}{l}
\zeta^2=c_r^2=1,\\
\zeta\text{ central},\\
[c_r,c_s]=\zeta\quad(r\ne s)
\end{array}
\right\rangle.
\tag{6.1}
\]

### Lemma 6.1. Each \(P_i\) is finite of order \(2^9=512\), and \(\zeta\ne1\).

**Proof.** The defining relations allow every word to be reordered into

\[
\zeta^\varepsilon c_1^{\varepsilon_1}\cdots c_8^{\varepsilon_8},
\qquad \varepsilon,\varepsilon_r\in\{0,1\},
\tag{6.2}
\]

so \(|P_i|\le2^9\).

For the reverse inequality, fix the order \(1<\cdots<8\) and put

\[
C_8:=\mathbb F_2\times\mathbb F_2^8
\]

with multiplication

\[
(\epsilon,u)(\delta,v)
 =\bigl(\epsilon+\delta+\beta(u,v),u+v\bigr),
\qquad
\beta(u,v)=\sum_{r>s}u_rv_s.
\tag{6.3}
\]

The bilinearity of \(\beta\) makes this associative. Put

\[
z=(1,0),\qquad d_r=(0,e_r).
\]

Then \(z\) is a central involution, every \(d_r\) is an involution, and for
\(r\ne s\),

\[
[d_r,d_s]=z.
\]

Thus (6.1) maps onto the group \(C_8\), which has \(2^9\) elements. Hence
\(|P_i|=2^9\), the normal forms (6.2) are unique, and the image of \(\zeta\)
is \(z\ne1\). \(\square\)

Since the graph has no edges between different blocks, the presentation
(4.5) is exactly the presentation of the free product of the \(P_i\)'s with
their common central subgroup \(\langle\zeta\rangle\) identified. Therefore

\[
\boxed{
N_E\cong *_{\langle\zeta\rangle,\ i\in I} P_i.
}
\tag{6.4}
\]

In particular \(\zeta=w\ne1\).

For a finite set of blocks \(J\subset I\), write

\[
M_J:=\langle P_i:i\in J\rangle
    \cong *_{\langle\zeta\rangle,\ i\in J}P_i.
\tag{6.5}
\]

The inclusions of finite subamalgams are injective by the normal-form theorem
for amalgamated free products.

## 7. Residual finiteness lemmas

We isolate two standard facts in the exact form needed below.

### Lemma 7.1. A free product of finitely many finite groups is residually finite.

**Proof.** Let

\[
F=A_1*\cdots *A_r
\]

with every \(A_i\) finite. Map \(F\) to the finite direct product
\(A_1\times\cdots\times A_r\) by sending \(A_i\) into its \(i\)-th factor.
Let \(K\) be the kernel. The restriction of this map to every conjugate of
every \(A_i\) is injective, so

\[
K\cap gA_i g^{-1}=1
\]

for all \(g\in F\). Equivalently, in the Bass--Serre tree of the free
product, \(K\) has trivial vertex stabilizers, hence acts freely. Therefore
\(K\) is a free group. It has finite index in the finitely generated group
\(F\), so it is finitely generated and hence is a finite-rank free group. In
particular \(K\) is residually finite.

It remains only to pass residual finiteness from a finitely generated
finite-index subgroup to the whole group; that is Lemma 7.2 below. \(\square\)

### Lemma 7.2. If a finitely generated group contains a residually finite subgroup of finite index, then it is residually finite.

**Proof.** Let \(H\le G\) be finite index and residually finite, with \(G\)
finitely generated. Replacing \(H\) by its finite-index core, we may assume
\(H\triangleleft G\). Then \(H\) is finitely generated.

Let \(1\ne g\in G\). If \(g\notin H\), the finite quotient \(G/H\) separates
\(g\) from \(1\). Suppose \(g\in H\). Residual finiteness of \(H\) gives a
finite-index subgroup \(L_0\triangleleft H\) with \(g\notin L_0\). Put
\(m=[H:L_0]\). A finitely generated group has only finitely many subgroups of
a prescribed finite index, because such subgroups arise as stabilizers in
homomorphisms to a finite symmetric group. Let \(L\) be the intersection of
all subgroups of \(H\) of index \(m\). Then \(L\) is finite index,
characteristic in \(H\), and \(L\subseteq L_0\), so \(g\notin L\). Since
\(H\triangleleft G\) and \(L\) is characteristic in \(H\), one has
\(L\triangleleft G\). Hence the finite quotient \(G/L\) separates \(g\).
\(\square\)

We will also use the immediate consequences that subgroups and finite direct
products of residually finite groups are residually finite.

## 8. Every finite block subamalgam \(M_J\) is residually finite

This is the most delicate algebraic step, and it admits a clean proof that
does not require the full Karrass--Pietrowski--Solitar virtual-freeness
theorem.

Fix finite nonempty \(J\subset I\), and let \(S_J\) be the finite set of all
sites in the blocks of \(J\). Thus \(|S_J|=8|J|\).

First form the **full Clifford quotient** \(C(S_J)\): it has a central
involution \(z\), one involution \(d_s\) for every \(s\in S_J\), and

\[
[d_s,d_{s'}]=z\qquad(s\ne s').
\tag{8.1}
\]

Exactly as in Lemma 6.1, after choosing any ordering of \(S_J\), it has
\(2^{|S_J|+1}\) elements and \(z\ne1\).

Adding the missing cross-block relations to \(M_J\) gives a surjection

\[
q:M_J\twoheadrightarrow C(S_J),
\tag{8.2}
\]

with \(q(\zeta)=z\ne1\).

Second, quotient \(M_J\) by its central subgroup \(\langle\zeta\rangle\).
Since \(\zeta\) is central,

\[
\ker r=\langle\zeta\rangle
\tag{8.3}
\]

for the quotient map

\[
r:M_J\to M_J/\langle\zeta\rangle.
\]

Killing \(\zeta\) turns each block group into an elementary abelian
\(2\)-group:

\[
P_i/\langle\zeta\rangle\cong(C_2)^8.
\]

The common amalgamated subgroup disappears, so

\[
M_J/\langle\zeta\rangle
\cong *_{i\in J}(C_2)^8.
\tag{8.4}
\]

The group on the right is residually finite by Lemma 7.1.

Now

\[
\ker q\cap\ker r
\subseteq \ker q\cap\langle\zeta\rangle=1,
\tag{8.5}
\]

because \(q(\zeta)\ne1\). Therefore the diagonal map

\[
(q,r):M_J\hookrightarrow
C(S_J)\times\left(*_{i\in J}(C_2)^8\right)
\tag{8.6}
\]

is injective. The first factor is finite and the second is residually finite,
so the product is residually finite. Hence its subgroup \(M_J\) is residually
finite.

The case \(J=\varnothing\) is trivial. We have proved:

### Proposition 8.1.
For every finite \(J\subset I\), \(M_J\) is finitely generated and residually
finite.

## 9. Adding one telescope level preserves residual finiteness

Suppose now that \(J\subset I\) is finite and \(\Gamma_n\)-invariant.
Conjugation by \(\Gamma_n\) preserves \(M_J\). It permutes the finite set
\(S_J\) of its lamp generators and fixes \(\zeta\). Hence the homomorphism

\[
\Gamma_n\longrightarrow\operatorname{Aut}(M_J)
\tag{9.1}
\]

has finite image: it factors through the finite permutation group
\(\operatorname{Sym}(S_J)\).

Let \(\Lambda\) be its kernel. Then \(\Lambda\) has finite index in
\(\Gamma_n\) and centralizes \(M_J\). Thus

\[
M_J\times\Lambda
\le M_J\rtimes\Gamma_n
\tag{9.2}
\]

with finite index.

By Proposition 8.1, \(M_J\) is finitely generated and residually finite.
By Lemma 2.1, \(\Gamma_n\) is finitely generated and residually finite, so its
finite-index subgroup \(\Lambda\) is also finitely generated and residually
finite. Therefore \(M_J\times\Lambda\) is finitely generated and residually
finite. Lemma 7.2 now gives:

### Proposition 9.1.
If \(J\) is finite and \(\Gamma_n\)-invariant, then

\[
M_J\rtimes\Gamma_n
\]

is residually finite.

## 10. The telescope core is locally residually finite

Define

\[
E_T:=N_E\rtimes T.
\tag{10.1}
\]

This is a normal subgroup of \(E\).

### Proposition 10.1. Every finitely generated subgroup of \(E_T\) is residually finite.

**Proof.** Let \(H\le E_T\) be generated by
\(h_1,\dots,h_r\). Using the split decomposition, write

\[
h_j=p_jg_j,
\qquad p_j\in N_E,\quad g_j\in T.
\tag{10.2}
\]

Because \(T=\bigcup_n\Gamma_n\), there is one \(n\) with
\(g_1,\dots,g_r\in\Gamma_n\).

Each \(p_j\) is represented by a finite word in the block amalgam (6.4), so
only finitely many blocks occur in all the \(p_j\)'s. Let \(J_0\) be this
finite block set. Put

\[
J:=\Gamma_n\cdot J_0.
\tag{10.3}
\]

By Lemma 5.2, every \(\Gamma_n\)-orbit on blocks is finite; since \(J_0\) is
finite, \(J\) is finite. It is \(\Gamma_n\)-invariant by construction.

Now every \(p_j\in M_J\), every \(g_j\in\Gamma_n\), and
\(\Gamma_n\) normalizes \(M_J\). Hence

\[
H\le M_J\rtimes\Gamma_n.
\tag{10.4}
\]

The group on the right is residually finite by Proposition 9.1, and residual
finiteness passes to subgroups. Therefore \(H\) is residually finite.
\(\square\)

### Corollary 10.2. \(E_T\) is LEF and therefore sofic.

**Proof.** Let \(F\subset E_T\) be finite. The subgroup \(\langle F\rangle\)
is finitely generated and hence residually finite by Proposition 10.1. Choose
a finite quotient of \(\langle F\rangle\) that is injective on \(F\): one
separates simultaneously the finitely many nontrivial elements
\(f^{-1}f'\), \(f\ne f'\), by taking a product of finite quotients. The
restriction to \(F\) is an exact local embedding into a finite group. This is
precisely the LEF condition.

A finite group acts faithfully on itself by left multiplication, so every
finite LEF model is an exact permutation model. Hence LEF implies sofic.
\(\square\)

A useful sanity check is that this does **not** claim \(E_T\) itself is
residually finite. In fact the repository proves that \(w\) dies in every
finite quotient of \(E_T\). The quantifiers are different: each finite window
lies in some residually finite subgroup \(M_J\rtimes\Gamma_n\), but there is
no single compatible family of finite quotients of the whole union.

## 11. A self-contained soficity lemma for split \(\mathbb Z\)-extensions

We now prove the only permanence fact needed for the final step instead of
using it as a black box.

For permutations of a finite set \(\Omega\), write

\[
d_H(\sigma,\rho)
=\frac1{|\Omega|}\bigl|\{\omega:\sigma\omega\ne\rho\omega\}\bigr|
\]

for normalized Hamming distance.

### Lemma 11.1. If \(K\) is sofic and \(\theta\in\operatorname{Aut}(K)\), then
\(K\rtimes_\theta\mathbb Z\) is sofic.

**Proof.** Write the stable generator as \(s\), so

\[
sks^{-1}=\theta(k).
\]

Fix a finite test set \(F\subset K\rtimes_\theta\mathbb Z\) and
\(\varepsilon>0\). Enlarge \(F\) finitely if necessary so that all products
whose multiplicativity is to be tested are included. Write each relevant
element as

\[
k s^m
\]

and choose \(L\) with \(|m|\le L\) for all of them.

Choose an integer \(N>2L\) so large that

\[
\frac{L}{N}<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.
\tag{11.1}
\]

We will model the group on

\[
\Omega\times\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z.
\]

For every \(j\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}\), collect the finitely many elements of
\(K\) of the form \(\theta^{-j}(k)\) which occur when expanding the required
products. Because \(K\) is sofic, there is a map

\[
\sigma:K\to\operatorname{Sym}(\Omega)
\]

which is \(\delta\)-multiplicative on this finite collection and moves at
least a \((1-\delta)\)-fraction of \(\Omega\) for every nontrivial element of
the collection, where

\[
\delta<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.
\tag{11.2}
\]

Define, for \(k\in K\), a level-preserving permutation \(\rho(k)\) by

\[
\rho(k)(\omega,j)
 :=\bigl(\sigma(\theta^{-\bar j}(k))\omega,j\bigr),
\tag{11.3}
\]

where \(\bar j\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}\) is the chosen representative of the
cyclic coordinate. Let

\[
S(\omega,j)=(\omega,j+1).
\tag{11.4}
\]

Away from the wrap-around level, conjugating \(\rho(k)\) by \(S\) implements
\(\theta\) exactly at the level of the arguments of \(\sigma\). More
generally, for \(|m|\le L\),

\[
d_H\bigl(S^m\rho(k)S^{-m},\rho(\theta^m k)\bigr)
\le \frac{|m|}{N}.
\tag{11.5}
\]

Indeed, the two permutations agree on every cyclic level for which shifting
by \(m\) does not cross the cut between \(N-1\) and \(0\); there are at most
\(|m|\) exceptional levels.

Also, by the \(\delta\)-multiplicativity of \(\sigma\),

\[
d_H\bigl(\rho(k)\rho(\ell),\rho(k\ell)\bigr)\le\delta
\tag{11.6}
\]

for all pairs needed in the test.

Now define

\[
\Phi(ks^m):=\rho(k)S^m.
\tag{11.7}
\]

If \(g=ks^m\) and \(h=\ell s^n\), then

\[
gh=k\theta^m(\ell)s^{m+n}.
\]

Using bi-invariance of Hamming distance, (11.5), and (11.6),

\[
\begin{aligned}
d_H(\Phi(g)\Phi(h),\Phi(gh))
&=d_H\bigl(\rho(k)S^m\rho(\ell)S^n,
           \rho(k\theta^m(\ell))S^{m+n}\bigr)\\
&\le \frac{|m|}{N}+\delta\\
&<\frac{2\varepsilon}{3}<\varepsilon.
\end{aligned}
\tag{11.8}
\]

Thus multiplicativity holds on the chosen finite test set.

For separation, if \(ks^m\ne1\) and \(m\ne0\), then
\(0<|m|<N\), so \(\Phi(ks^m)\) changes the cyclic coordinate of every point;
hence

\[
d_H(\Phi(ks^m),1)=1.
\]

If \(m=0\), then \(k\ne1\). Every \(\theta^{-j}(k)\) is nontrivial, so by the
choice of \(\sigma\), \(\rho(k)\) moves at least a
\((1-\delta)\)-fraction on every level. Therefore

\[
d_H(\Phi(k),1)\ge1-\delta.
\]

Since \(\delta\) can be arbitrarily small, these are sofic approximations.
\(\square\)

## 12. Final assembly: \(E\) is sofic

From (4.1),

\[
E=N_E\rtimes V.
\]

From (2.3),

\[
V=T\rtimes\langle\tau\rangle.
\]

Hence

\[
E_T=N_E\rtimes T\triangleleft E,
\qquad
E/E_T\cong\mathbb Z,
\tag{12.1}
\]

and the extension splits along the stable letter \(t\) (equivalently
\(\tau\)). Thus

\[
E\cong E_T\rtimes\mathbb Z
\tag{12.2}
\]

for the automorphism of \(E_T\) induced by conjugation by \(t\).

Corollary 10.2 gives that \(E_T\) is sofic. Lemma 11.1 therefore applies and
yields

\[
\boxed{E\text{ is sofic}.}
\]

This proves the theorem. \(\square\)

# Consequences

## 13. Finitely presented sofic but not MF

The non-MF theorem for the literal presentation is already proved in the
manuscript and its formal development: the central involution

\[
w=[tct^{-1},v_1(tct^{-1})v_1^{-1}]\ne1
\]

is killed by every homomorphism from \(E\) into a unitary norm-matrix corona.
Therefore \(E\) is not MF.

Combining that result with the theorem above gives

\[
\boxed{
E\text{ is finitely presented, sofic, and not MF}.
}
\]

Since every sofic group is hyperlinear,

\[
\boxed{
E\text{ is also a finitely presented hyperlinear non-MF group}.
}
\]

This strengthens the manuscript's separate finitely generated witness \(W\):
the source group \(E\) itself already has the desired sofic/non-MF
separation.

## 14. The sofic radical is trivial

Define the sofic radical as the intersection of the kernels of all
homomorphisms from a group to sofic groups. Since \(E\) itself is sofic, the
identity map

\[
\operatorname{id}_E:E\to E
\]

is one of those homomorphisms. Hence

\[
\operatorname{Rad}_{\mathrm{sof}}(E)=1.
\tag{14.1}
\]

On the other hand the already established operator-norm obstruction gives

\[
1\ne w\in\operatorname{Rad}_{\mathrm{MF}}(E).
\tag{14.2}
\]

Thus, on this single finitely presented group,

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Rad}_{\mathrm{sof}}(E)=1
\quad<\quad
\operatorname{Rad}_{\mathrm{MF}}(E).
}
\]

# Audit notes

## 15. Why the proof does not use a false permanence statement

One earlier attempted route considered the quotient \(E\to W\) and the kernel
\(\ker(E\to W)\). That kernel is a free group of countably infinite rank, not
amenable. More importantly, even an amenable kernel with a sofic quotient
would not give the extension theorem needed here.

The proof above uses the valid direction in a much more concrete form:

- first prove the normal subgroup \(E_T\) itself is sofic;
- then adjoin the amenable quotient \(\mathbb Z\).

Section 11 proves the needed split-\(\mathbb Z\) case directly, so no
extension-permanence ambiguity remains.

## 16. Why this does not contradict the fact that \(w\) dies in every finite quotient

The groups

\[
M_J\rtimes\Gamma_n
\]

are finite-window containers inside \(E_T\). They are residually finite, and
their finite quotients can keep \(w\) nontrivial. They are **not** finite
quotients of the whole group \(E\).

When the stable letter is added, soficity uses approximate permutation models,
not homomorphisms to finite groups. The cyclic truncation in Section 11 is
exact on almost every level but intentionally fails at a vanishing fraction of
wrap-around levels. That is precisely how a sofic approximation can see \(w\)
even though every exact finite quotient of \(E\) kills it.

The mathematical obstruction that originally made the sofic proof
