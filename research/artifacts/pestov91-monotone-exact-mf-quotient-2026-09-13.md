# Deleting the transition orbit computes the entire MF quotient

2026-09-13. Written proof, not Lean-verified. The preceding dichotomy
showed that the monotone binary subshift gives a non-MF elementary group
in rank at least four. Here its complete MF radical and maximal MF
quotient are computed, including the finite residual.

## Theorem

Let `X` be the binary subshift forbidding `10`, let

`R=LC(X,F_2) semidirect Z`, `G_n=EL_n(R)`,
`A=F_2[z,z^{-1}]`.

For every `n>=4`, restriction to the two fixed points induces an exact
sequence

`1 -> L -> G_n -> SL_n(A) x SL_n(A) -> 1`,               (1)

where

`L ~= GL_fin(Z x {1,...,n},F_2) ~= SL_infinity(F_2)`

uses the usual inclusions by an identity block. In particular `L` is
infinite, simple and locally finite. Moreover

`Rad_MF(G_n)=Res_fin(G_n)=L`.                            (2)

The quotient in (1) is residually finite and is the maximal MF quotient:
every homomorphism from `G_n` into an MF group factors uniquely through
the endpoint map. Thus the dynamical operation of deleting the single
transition orbit realizes this universal group quotient exactly.

For `n=4`, `G_4` is a non-MF Kazhdan group generated by four torsion
elements of orders `7,2,2,2`. Its MF radical is itself LEF, since it is
locally finite. The finite residual and the exact sequence in (1)
already have the same description for `n>=3`; the MF-radical conclusion
here uses `n>=4`.

## 1. The orbit and the coefficient ring

Let `z_i=0` for `i<0` and `z_i=1` for `i>=0`, and write `y_j=T^j z`.
Then

`X={0^infinity,1^infinity} union {y_j:j in Z}`,

with `y_j -> 0^infinity` as `j -> -infinity` and
`y_j -> 1^infinity` as `j -> +infinity`. Every point `y_j` is isolated.
The orbit is dense, and this identifies `X` with the two-end
compactification of `Z`.

Consequently a locally constant function is exactly a sequence
`f(j) in F_2` that is eventually constant at each end, with possibly
different endpoint values `f_-` and `f_+`. Indeed continuity at the
endpoints forces these tails to be constant, while any such sequence
extends continuously to the two endpoints. Put `p=1_(x_0=1)` as a
function on the subshift. On the indexed orbit it is `p(j)=1_(j>=0)`.
The coefficient automorphism is `alpha(f)(j)=f(j-1)`.

On `V=F_2^(Z)`, let `u e_j=e_(j+1)` and let a function act diagonally.
This gives a faithful representation of `R`. In fact the term `f_k u^k`
in a crossed-product normal form has entries only in positions
`(j+k,j)`, and its entry there is `f_k(j+k)`. Different exponents occupy
different diagonals. If the represented matrix is zero, every `f_k`
vanishes on the dense orbit and hence on `X`.

Let

`e=p-upu^{-1}=1_(x_(-1)=0,x_0=1)`.

It is the diagonal projection onto `e_0` in this representation. For
all integers `i,j`, define `E_ij=u^i e u^{-j}`. These are the ordinary
finite-support matrix units on `V`.

## 2. The endpoint ring sequence

Endpoint evaluation is the unital ring epimorphism

`sigma:R -> A x A`,
`sigma(sum_k f_k u^k)=(sum_k f_(k,-)z^k, sum_k f_(k,+)z^k)`.          (3)

It is multiplicative because the shift fixes the two endpoints. It is
onto: `p` separates the endpoints and `u` maps to `(z,z)`, so arbitrary
Laurent polynomials in the two components can be lifted independently.

Its kernel is exactly

`I=M_fin(Z,F_2)` in the faithful representation above.               (4)

If the two symbols vanish, uniqueness of Laurent coefficients says
that each coefficient has zero endpoint values, hence finite support
on the orbit. There are finitely many degrees, so the represented
operator has only finitely many nonzero matrix entries. Conversely
every finite-support matrix is a sum of the `E_ij`, and these have zero
endpoint symbols. In particular `I=ReR`.

The choice of the prime field `F_2` matters in the group calculation:
every invertible finite matrix over it has determinant one. No assertion
about determinant corrections over larger finite fields is made here.

## 3. Identify the entire group kernel, not just relative roots

The epimorphism (3) is onto on elementary groups. Since `A` is a
Euclidean Laurent polynomial ring, elementary reduction gives
`EL_n(A)=SL_n(A)` for `n>=3`. Thus the image is the full product in (1).

A group element in the kernel is `I_n+K` with `K in M_n(I)`. Viewed on
`V^n`, it is the identity outside a finite coordinate set and preserves
that finite coordinate block. Its inverse exists, so the finite block
is invertible. Therefore the group kernel is contained in
`GL_fin(Z x {1,...,n},F_2)`.

For the reverse inclusion, every invertible finite block over `F_2` is
generated by finite elementary transvections. A transvection between
coordinates `(a,i)` and `(b,j)` with `a!=b` is exactly the root
`e_ab(E_ij)` in `EL_n(R)`. If `a=b` and `i!=j`, use another outer colour
`c` and any integer `k`. The commutator of the transvections from
`(c,k)` to `(a,i)` and from `(a,j)` to `(c,k)` is the desired
transvection from `(a,j)` to `(a,i)`. Both inputs have distinct outer
colours and already belong to `EL_n(R)`. This proves the reverse
inclusion and the exact identification of the group kernel in (1).

This argument treats all units in `1+M_n(I)`. It does not assume that
the kernel of an elementary quotient is always the relative elementary
subgroup, which would leave an additional kernel to check in general.

Local finiteness of `L` follows from finite support. For simplicity,
take a nonidentity element in a normal subgroup of `L` and place it in
a finite `SL_m(F_2)`, `m>=3`. Intersection with that finite simple group
is nontrivial and normal, so contains it. The same argument applies in
every larger finite block. Their union is `L`, proving simplicity.

## 4. Residual finiteness of the endpoint quotient

Any nonzero Laurent polynomial over `F_2` has a nonzero value at some
nonzero element of a finite extension of `F_2`. To see this, multiply
by a power of `z` and avoid the finitely many roots of the resulting
nonzero polynomial in the infinite algebraic closure. The chosen
element belongs to a finite extension.

For a nonidentity matrix in `SL_n(A)`, apply such an evaluation to a
nonzero entry of its difference from the identity. The resulting finite
group image is nonidentity. Hence `SL_n(A)`, and its direct square, are
residually finite and therefore MF.

Any homomorphism from `G_n` to a finite group kills `L`: its restriction
to the infinite simple group `L` is either injective or trivial, and
injectivity into a finite group is impossible. The residually finite
quotient separates every element outside `L`. This proves
`Res_fin(G_n)=L` for every `n>=3`.

## 5. The MF radical and its universal quotient

The previous dichotomy constructs

`s=up+1-p`, `t=pu^{-1}+1-p`, `ts=1`, `1-st=e!=0`.

The printed one-auxiliary-coordinate torsion-defect theorem therefore
puts the nontrivial root `e_12(e)` in `Rad_MF(G_n)` for `n>=4`.
This root belongs to `L`. Since `L` is simple, its normal closure
inside `L`, hence inside `G_n`, is exactly `L`. The MF radical is
normal, so `L<=Rad_MF(G_n)`.

Conversely the endpoint quotient is MF by section 4. Its quotient map
is one of the homomorphisms defining the MF radical, and has kernel
`L`. Thus `Rad_MF(G_n)<=L`, proving (2).

Every homomorphism into an MF group now kills `L` and hence factors
uniquely through (1). The target of (1) is itself MF, so this is the
maximal MF quotient, not merely a particular residually finite image.
The four torsion generators and property (T) for `G_4` are the results
of the preceding subshift dichotomy. No conclusion about soficity or
hyperlinearity of the extension is needed or asserted.
