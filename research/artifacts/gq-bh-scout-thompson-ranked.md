**15.19 (L. Bartholdi, S. Sidki).**

> Let p be a prime, and 𝓕_p the class of finitely generated groups acting faithfully on a p-regular
> rooted tree by finite automata. Any group in 𝓕_p is residually-p (residually in the class of finite
> p-groups) and has word problem that is solvable in (at worst) exponential time. There exist therefore
> groups that are residually-p, have a solvable word problem, and do not belong to 𝓕_p; though no
> concrete example is known. For instance:
> a) Is it true that some (or even all) the groups given in (R. I. Grigorchuk, Math. USSR–Sb., 54
> (1986), 185–205) do not belong to 𝓕_p when the sequence ω is computable, but not periodic?
> b) Does ℤ≀(ℤ≀ℤ) belong to 𝓕_2? (Here the wreath products are restricted.)
> See (A. M. Brunner, S. Sidki, J. Algebra, 257 (2002), 51–64) and (R. I. Grigorchuk, V. V.
> Nekrashevich, V. I. Sushchanskiĭ, in: Dynamical systems, automata, and infinite groups. Proc. Steklov
> Inst. Math., 231 (2000), 128–203).

**Lead for (a), unchecked.** The problem itself says every group in 𝓕_p has word problem solvable in
exponential time, and that holds for every faithful finite-automaton action, not only the standard one.
So a lower bound on the complexity of WP(G_ω) that exceeds every exponential would answer "some"
affirmatively. That would put G_ω outside 𝓕_p for every p at once; changing the generating set only
changes word length by a constant factor. The plan:
1. **Detecting words.** Show there are words w_k of length 2^{O(k)} such that w_k = 1 in G_ω iff
   ω_k equals a given symbol, with w_k computable from k and from ω_1, …, ω_{k−1}. This is the
   quantitative content of Grigorchuk's theorem that WP(G_ω) is solvable iff ω is computable. Check
   whether his 1984/86 proof already gives the length bound. The natural candidates are commutators
   supported on the vertex 1^{k−1}0, whose b, c, d sections depend on ω_k.
2. **Time hierarchy.** Choose a computable ω, non-periodic and with every symbol occurring infinitely
   often, such that ω_k cannot be computed in time 2^{2^{2^k}}. The time hierarchy theorem gives one.
3. **Conclusion.** WP(G_ω) is then not solvable in time 2^{O(n)}, so G_ω ∉ 𝓕_p for every p.

The main risk is step 1: detecting words might need length 2^{2^k}, and then the construction in
step 2 still works but needs a faster-growing time bound. Check the literature first, in particular
later work on G_ω by Grigorchuk, and by Bartholdi and Erschler (growth and word problem).
Related on main: `finite-recursion-gives-no-computable-depth-bound` (e6f7e2b3d7, refereed), which
gives the depth criterion for functionally recursive groups.

**18.47 (S. Kohl).**

> Is it algorithmically decidable whether a group generated by three given class transpositions
> (for the definition, see 17.57)
> a) has only finite orbits on ℤ?
> b) acts transitively on the set of nonnegative integers in its support?
> A difficult case is the group ⟨τ_{1(2),4(6)}, τ_{1(3),2(6)}, τ_{2(3),4(6)}⟩, which acts transitively
> on ℕ ∖ 0(6) if and only if Collatz' 3n+1 conjecture is true.

**Leads for 18.47(a).**
- **The d-adic case.** When all six moduli are powers of one integer d, the group acts on a regular
  rooted tree by cylinder swaps. Here the prefix and nesting combinatorics of
  `kourovka-18-48-for-d-adic-class-transpositions` may make "only finite orbits" decidable. For two
  generators, the node's lemmas bound the finite components by 4 vertices, which suggests a decision
  procedure (not written out). Three generators need a new idea.
- **The general case.** Undecidability would need a three-generator simulation of a counter machine.
  bh-free-58's barrier nodes (4f611e3b3; `ct-z-finite-cycle-problems-are-undecidable`) use arbitrary
  elements of CT(ℤ), so the three-generator restriction is the entire difficulty. A first test: can a
  product of three class transpositions simulate one step of a reversible two-counter machine?
- **The Collatz example.** It shows that part (b) for three generators is at least Collatz-hard. Part (a)
  has no printed hard instance.

## C. Lesson

The class-transposition problems split along the same line as the rest of the CT(Z) cluster:
- **One base** (moduli powers of one integer, i.e. a tree): the proof is word combinatorics. For
  18.48 the five-chain lemma in the new node does it.
- **Two independent bases:** classes meet transversally, and the open part of every problem sits
  exactly there.
