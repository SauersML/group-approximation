# Bernoulli collapse is null or eventually total (2026-09-16)

Proof artifact for `bernoulli-collapse-is-null-or-eventually-total`, cited by the route
`bernoulli-collapse-is-null-or-eventually-total-proof`.

## 0. Setting and imported facts

`G` is a countably infinite group and `s = h^Rok_sup(G) ∈ [0, ∞]`. For `h ∈ [0, ∞]`, `X_h` is a Bernoulli shift
over `G` with base entropy `h`. Its isomorphism class depends only on `h` (Seward, arXiv:1805.08279v1,
Theorem 1.1, imported as `bernoulli-shifts-with-equal-base-entropy-are-isomorphic`). `X_0` is a one-point
system, and `X_h` is non-atomic for `h > 0`.

**Notation.**
- For `a, b ∈ (0, ∞)` write `a ~ b` when `X_a ≅ X_b`. This is an equivalence relation on `(0, ∞)`, with classes
  `C_a = {b : a ~ b}`.
- `D(G)` and `W_d` are as in `bernoulli-isomorphism-collapse-set-is-a-subgroup`. "Item n" below refers to that
  claim. Since `X_0` is isomorphic only to itself, `D(G) = {0} ∪ {b − a : a ~ b}`.
- The **own-square set** is `S(G) = {h ∈ (0, ∞) : X_h ≅ X_h × X_h}`. By Item 1, `X_h × X_h ≅ X_(2h)`, so
  `S(G) = {h : h ~ 2h}`.
- A level `a > 0` is **rigid** when `C_a = {a}`. The **non-rigid set** is `R(G) = {a > 0 : C_a ≠ {a}}`, and
  `r(G) = inf R(G) ∈ [0, ∞]`, with `inf ∅ = ∞`.

**Facts used.**
- **(F1) Translation** (Item 1). If `a ~ b` and `t ≥ 0`, then `a + t ~ b + t`.
- **(F2) Subgroup** (Item 3). `D(G)` is an additive subgroup of `R`, so `D(G) = −D(G)`.
- **(F3) Threshold** (Item 6). If `0 ≠ d ∈ D(G)` and `h ∈ W_d`, then `min(h, h + d) ≥ s`.
- **(F4) Analytic sets.** Continuous images, and Borel preimages, of analytic subsets of Polish spaces are
  analytic. Analytic sets are universally measurable (Lusin), and they have the Baire property
  (Lusin–Sierpiński). Source: A. S. Kechris, *Classical Descriptive Set Theory*, GTM 156, Springer 1995,
  Sections 14, 21 and 29. The Lusin–Souslin theorem used in Step 2 (a Borel injection of a Polish space has
  Borel image and Borel inverse) is in Section 15 of the same book. The theorem numbers were not rechecked on
  2026-09-16 and are unverified here.
- **(F5) Steinhaus and Pettis–Piccard.** Let `A ⊆ R`. If `A` is Lebesgue measurable with positive measure, or if
  `A` has the Baire property and is non-meager, then `A − A` contains an interval `(−δ, δ)` with `δ > 0`.
  Source: Kechris, loc. cit., the Pettis theorem in Section 9 (number unverified). For measure this is
  Steinhaus's classical theorem.

## 1. The isomorphism relation on base entropies is analytic

**Lemma 1.** The relation `~` is an analytic subset of `(0, ∞)^2`. Hence the following are analytic, so they are
Lebesgue measurable and have the Baire property: each class `C_a`, the set `D(G)`, each `W_d ∩ (0, ∞)`, the set
`S(G)`, and the set `R(G)`.

**Step 1: a continuous family of Bernoulli measures.**
- Let `C = N ∪ {∞}` be the one-point compactification of `N = {0, 1, 2, ...}`. It is compact, metrizable and
  zero-dimensional. Its clopen sets are the finite subsets of `N` and their complements in `C`.
- Let `Ω = C^G` with the product topology and the shift `(g·x)(k) = x(g^-1 k)`. It is compact, metrizable and
  zero-dimensional, and `G` acts by homeomorphisms.
- Let `𝒜` be the algebra generated by the cylinders `{x : x(g) ∈ U}`, for `g ∈ G` and `U ⊆ C` clopen. It is
  countable, consists of clopen sets, is a base of the topology, and generates the Borel σ-algebra. Enumerate it
  as `A_1, A_2, …`. The clopen algebra `𝒜_2` of `Ω × Ω` generated by products of members of `𝒜` is also countable.
- `Prob(Ω)` and `Prob(Ω × Ω)` carry the weak* topology, so they are compact metrizable. For a clopen `U`, the map
  `µ ↦ µ(U)` is continuous, because `1_U` is continuous.
- For `m ∈ (0, ∞)` let `γ_m` be the geometric law `γ_m(n) = (m+1)^-1 (m/(m+1))^n` on `N`, a measure on `C` with
  `γ_m({∞}) = 0`. Its mean is `m`. Its Shannon entropy is
  `log(m+1) + m·log((m+1)/m) = (m+1)log(m+1) − m log m =: φ(m)`.
- `φ'(m) = log((m+1)/m) > 0`, `φ(m) → 0` as `m → 0`, and `φ(m) ≥ log(m+1) → ∞`. So `φ` is an increasing
  homeomorphism of `(0, ∞)`.
- Put `λ_h = γ_(φ^-1(h))` and `β(h) = λ_h^G ∈ Prob(Ω)`. Then `β(h)` is a Bernoulli shift with base entropy `h`,
  so it is a model of `X_h`.

`β` is continuous.
- For a rectangle `∩_(g ∈ F) {x : x(g) ∈ U_g}`, with `F` finite and each `U_g` clopen, the `β(h)`-measure is
  `∏_g λ_h(U_g)`.
- Each `λ_h(U_g)` is a finite sum of values `γ_m(n)`, or one minus such a sum. So it is continuous in `m`, hence
  in `h`.
- Every member of `𝒜` is a finite disjoint union of rectangles, so `h ↦ β(h)(A)` is continuous for `A ∈ 𝒜`.
- The linear span of `{1_A : A ∈ 𝒜}` is a unital algebra of continuous functions separating points, so it is
  uniformly dense in `C(Ω)` by Stone–Weierstrass. Hence `h ↦ ∫ f dβ(h)` is continuous for each `f ∈ C(Ω)`.

**Step 2: isomorphism is the projection of a Borel set of joinings.** Let `J` be the set of triples
`(µ, ν, ρ) ∈ Prob(Ω) × Prob(Ω) × Prob(Ω × Ω)` such that:
- **(J1)** `ρ(A × Ω) = µ(A)` and `ρ(Ω × A) = ν(A)` for every `A ∈ 𝒜`;
- **(J2)** `ρ(gU) = ρ(U)` for every `g ∈ G` and `U ∈ 𝒜_2`, for the diagonal action;
- **(J3)** for all `i, n ≥ 1` there is `j` with `ρ((A_i × Ω) Δ (Ω × A_j)) < 1/n`, and for all `i, n ≥ 1` there is
  `j` with `ρ((Ω × A_i) Δ (A_j × Ω)) < 1/n`.

(J1) and (J2) are countably many closed conditions. Each set `(A_i × Ω) Δ (Ω × A_j)` is clopen, so (J3) is a
countable intersection of countable unions of open conditions. So `J` is Borel.

**Claim.** For shift-invariant `µ, ν ∈ Prob(Ω)`, the systems `(Ω, µ)` and `(Ω, ν)` are isomorphic iff
`(µ, ν, ρ) ∈ J` for some `ρ`.

*(⇒)* Let `T` be an isomorphism: a Borel, `G`-equivariant, measure-preserving map, defined `µ`-a.e., with an
a.e. inverse. Put `ρ = (id × T)_* µ`.
- (J1) holds, since `ρ(Ω × A) = µ(T^-1 A) = ν(A)`.
- (J2) holds, since `ρ(gU) = µ{x : (g^-1 x, T g^-1 x) ∈ U} = ρ(U)` by equivariance and invariance of `µ`.
- (J3) holds. The algebra `𝒜` generates the Borel σ-algebra, so for `i, n` there is `j` with
  `ν(T A_i Δ A_j) < 1/n`, and then `ρ((A_i × Ω) Δ (Ω × A_j)) = µ(A_i Δ T^-1 A_j) < 1/n`. Choosing `j` with
  `µ(T^-1 A_i Δ A_j) < 1/n` gives the second half.

*(⇐)* Let `(µ, ν, ρ) ∈ J`.
- By (J1), (J2) and uniqueness of measures agreeing on a generating algebra, `ρ` is a `G`-invariant coupling of
  `µ` and `ν`.
- Let `Σ̄_1` be the family of Borel `V ⊆ Ω × Ω` with `ρ(V Δ (E × Ω)) = 0` for some Borel `E ⊆ Ω`. It is a
  σ-algebra.
- It is closed under `ρ`-limits. Suppose `ρ(V Δ V_n) → 0` with `V_n ∈ Σ̄_1`, say `V_n = E_n × Ω` mod `ρ`. Pass to a
  subsequence with `Σ_n ρ(V Δ V_n) < ∞`. By Borel–Cantelli, `V = limsup_n (E_n × Ω) = (limsup_n E_n) × Ω` mod `ρ`.
- By the second half of (J3), every `Ω × A_i` lies in `Σ̄_1`. The Borel sets `E` with `Ω × E ∈ Σ̄_1` form a
  σ-algebra containing `𝒜`, so it contains every Borel set. Symmetrically, by the first half of (J3), every
  `E × Ω` agrees `ρ`-a.e. with a set of the form `Ω × F`.
- **Building `T`.** Choose Borel `F_i` with `ρ((Ω × A_i) Δ (F_i × Ω)) = 0`. Put `Φ(x) = (1_(F_i)(x))_i` and
  `Ψ(y) = (1_(A_i)(y))_i`, maps `Ω → {0,1}^N`.
  - `𝒜` separates points, so `Ψ` is a Borel injection. By Lusin–Souslin, `Ψ(Ω)` is Borel and `Ψ^-1` is Borel on it.
  - `Ψ(π_2 ω) = Φ(π_1 ω)` for `ρ`-a.e. `ω`, so `Φ(x) ∈ Ψ(Ω)` for `µ`-a.e. `x`.
  - Put `T = Ψ^-1 ∘ Φ` there, and constant elsewhere. Then `π_2 = T ∘ π_1` `ρ`-a.e.
  - Symmetrically there is a Borel `S` with `π_1 = S ∘ π_2` `ρ`-a.e.
- **Properties of `T`.**
  - `µ(T^-1 E) = ρ{ω : π_2 ω ∈ E} = ν(E)`.
  - `S T π_1 = S π_2 = π_1` `ρ`-a.e., so `ST = id` `µ`-a.e. Likewise `TS = id` `ν`-a.e.
  - **Equivariance.** Let `Z = {ω : T π_1 ω = π_2 ω}`, which is `ρ`-conull. Since `ρ` is invariant, `Z ∩ g^-1 Z` is
    conull. For `ω` in it, `T(g π_1 ω) = π_2(gω) = g π_2 ω = g T(π_1 ω)`.

So `T` is an isomorphism, and the Claim holds.

**Step 3.** `Iso = {(µ, ν) : ∃ρ, (µ, ν, ρ) ∈ J}` is the projection of a Borel set, hence analytic. So
`~ = (β × β)^-1(Iso)` is analytic by (F4).
- `C_a` is the preimage of `~` under `b ↦ (a, b)`.
- `W_d ∩ (0, ∞)` is the preimage of `~` under `h ↦ (h, h + d)`, on the open set `{h > 0, h + d > 0}`.
- `S(G)` is the preimage under `h ↦ (h, 2h)`.
- `D(G) = {0} ∪ (image of ~ under (a, b) ↦ b − a)`.
- `R(G)` is the projection of `~` minus the diagonal.

All five are analytic. ∎

## 2. The dichotomy

**Theorem 2.** Let `G` be countably infinite. The following are equivalent:
- **(i)** `D(G) = R`;
- **(ii)** `D(G)` has positive Lebesgue measure;
- **(ii')** `D(G)` is non-meager;
- **(iii)** some class `C_a` has positive Lebesgue measure;
- **(iii')** some class `C_a` is non-meager;
- **(iv)** `~` has positive planar Lebesgue measure;
- **(v)** there is `M < ∞` with `X_h ≅ X_(h')` for all `h, h' ∈ [M, ∞)`;
- **(vi)** `S(G)` has positive Lebesgue measure;
- **(vi')** `S(G)` is non-meager.

When they fail, `D(G)`, `S(G)` and every class `C_a` are Lebesgue-null and meager, and `~` is planar-null.

*Proof.*

**(v) ⇒ (i).** For `d ≥ 0` we have `M ~ M + d`, so `d ∈ D(G)`. Then `D(G) = R` by (F2).

**(i) ⇒ (ii), (ii').** Trivial.

**(ii) ⇒ (iii) and (ii') ⇒ (iii').**
- By (F2), `D(G)` is symmetric. So if `D(G)` has positive measure, or is non-meager, the same holds for
  `D(G) ∩ (0, ∞)`.
- Let `d > 0` lie in `D(G)`. Some `h ∈ [0, ∞)` has `X_h ≅ X_(h+d)`, and `h ≠ 0` because `X_0` is a point. By (F1),
  `n ~ n + d` for every integer `n ≥ h`.
- So `D(G) ∩ (0, ∞) = ∪_(n ≥ 1) E_n`, with `E_n = {d > 0 : n ~ n + d} ⊆ C_n − n`.
- A countable union of null sets is null, and of meager sets is meager. So some `E_n`, and hence `C_n`, has
  positive measure, or is non-meager.

**(iii) ⇒ (v) and (iii') ⇒ (v).**
- `C_a = ∪_n (C_a ∩ (0, n])`, so some `A = C_a ∩ (0, n]` has positive measure, or is non-meager. `A` is analytic
  (Lemma 1), so it has the Baire property.
- By (F5) there is `δ > 0` with `(−δ, δ) ⊆ A − A`.
- Let `z ≥ n` and `e ∈ (0, δ)`. Pick `b_1, b_2 ∈ A` with `b_2 − b_1 = e`. Both are `~ a`, so `b_1 ~ b_2`.
- `t = z − b_1 ≥ 0`, since `b_1 ≤ n ≤ z`. By (F1), `z = b_1 + t ~ b_2 + t = z + e`.
- Given `n ≤ h < h'`, choose `k` with `(h' − h)/k < δ`. Then `h ~ h + (h' − h)/k ~ … ~ h'`. So (v) holds with
  `M = n`.

**(v) ⇒ (iii), (iv), (vi), (vi').** The sets `C_M ⊇ [M, ∞)`, `~ ⊇ [M, ∞)^2` and `S(G) ⊇ [M, ∞)` are non-null
and non-meager.

**(iv) ⇒ (iii).** `~` is analytic, hence Lebesgue measurable. By Fubini, some section `C_a` has positive measure.

**(vi) ⇒ (v) and (vi') ⇒ (v).**
- Choose `n` with `A = S(G) ∩ (0, n]` of positive measure, or non-meager, and take `δ` from (F5).
- For `b ∈ S(G)` and `u ≥ b`, apply (F1) with `t = u − b` to `b ~ 2b`. This gives `u ~ u + b`.
- Let `e ∈ (0, δ)`, and pick `b_1, b_2 ∈ A` with `b_2 − b_1 = e`. For `u ≥ n` we get `u ~ u + b_1` and
  `u ~ u + b_2`, so `u + b_1 ~ u + b_2`.
- So `z ~ z + e` for every `z ≥ n + b_1`, in particular for every `z ≥ 2n`. Chaining as above gives (v) with
  `M = 2n`.

**When the conditions fail.** Each negated condition is exactly the null or meager statement recorded. ∎

## 3. Non-rigid levels

**Proposition 3.** Let `R = R(G)` and `r = r(G)`.
- **(a)** `R` is upward closed. So `R` is `(r, ∞)` or `[r, ∞)`, or empty.
- **(b)** `R ⊆ [s, ∞)`, so `r ≥ s`.
- **(c)** `R ≠ ∅` iff `D(G) ≠ {0}` iff `r < ∞`.
- **(d)** If `a ~ b` with `a < b`, then `a ~ a + k(b − a)` for every integer `k ≥ 0`. Every class `C_a` with
  `a ∈ R` is unbounded above.
- **(e)** Under the conditions of Theorem 2, `R` is a single class, namely `C_M`. So all `X_a` with `a ∈ R` are
  isomorphic, `R ⊇ (r, ∞)`, and every level in `(0, r)` is rigid.
- **(f)** When the conditions of Theorem 2 fail, every class is null and meager, including the classes of
  non-rigid levels.

*Proof.*
- **(a)** If `a ~ b ≠ a` and `t ≥ 0`, then `a + t ~ b + t ≠ a + t` by (F1).
- **(b)** If `a ~ b` with `a ≠ b`, then `d = b − a ∈ D(G) \ {0}` and `a ∈ W_d`. By (F3), `a ≥ min(a, b) ≥ s`.
- **(c)** `R ≠ ∅` iff some `b − a ≠ 0` lies in `D(G)`.
- **(d)** By induction on `k`: (F1) with `t = k(b − a)` gives `a + k(b − a) ~ b + k(b − a) = a + (k+1)(b − a)`.
  - Let `a ∈ R`, with `a ~ b ≠ a`. If `b > a`, the progression lies in `C_a`.
  - If `b < a`, apply the progression to `b ~ a`. Then `C_a = C_b` contains `b + k(a − b)` for all `k`.
- **(e)** Let `a ∈ R`. By (d), `C_a` contains some `c ≥ M`, and `c ~ M` by (v). So `a ∈ C_M`. Conversely `C_M`
  contains `[M, ∞)`, so it is not a singleton and `C_M ⊆ R`. The rest follows from (a).
- **(f)** This is Theorem 2. ∎

## 4. Criteria for collapse above the supremum

**Theorem 4.** Let `s = h^Rok_sup(G) < ∞`. The following are equivalent:
- **(T1)** `X_h ≅ X_(h')` for all `s < h < h' < ∞`;
- **(T2)** `D(G) = R` and `r(G) = s`;
- **(T2')** there is `M < ∞` such that all `X_h` with `h ≥ M` are isomorphic, and each `X_a` with `a > s` is
  isomorphic to some `X_b` with `b ≠ a`;
- **(T3)** for every `ε > 0`, the set `S(G) ∩ (s, s + ε)` has positive Lebesgue measure;
- **(T3')** for every `ε > 0`, the set `S(G) ∩ (s, s + ε)` is non-meager;
- **(T4)** there is `η > 0` with `X_h ≅ X_h × X_h` for every `h ∈ (s, s + η)`.

Through Seward's Theorem 1.1, (T1) is the claim `bernoulli-shifts-above-rokhlin-supremum-are-isomorphic` for
`G`. A base `(L, λ)` with `H(λ) = h` gives `L^G ≅ X_h`.

*Proof.*
- **(T1) ⇒ (T4).** For `h > s` we have `s < h < 2h < ∞`, so `h ~ 2h`, which is `X_h ≅ X_h × X_h`. Any `η` works.
- **(T4) ⇒ (T3), (T3').** A non-empty open interval is non-null and non-meager.
- **(T3) ⇒ (T2') and (T3') ⇒ (T2').**
  - `S(G)` is non-null, or non-meager, so Theorem 2 (vi) or (vi') gives the first half of (T2').
  - Let `a > s`. The set `S(G) ∩ (s, a)` is non-null, or non-meager, so it contains some `h`.
  - (F1) with `t = a − h ≥ 0`, applied to `h ~ 2h`, gives `a ~ a + h`, and `a + h ≠ a`.
- **(T2') ⇔ (T2).** The first halves agree by Theorem 2. For the second halves, "every `a > s` is non-rigid"
  says `R ⊇ (s, ∞)`, which is `r ≤ s`, and `r ≥ s` always holds by Proposition 3(b).
- **(T2) ⇒ (T1).** By Proposition 3(e), `R` is a single class. By `r = s` and Proposition 3(a), `R ⊇ (s, ∞)`. ∎

**Reading.** (T4) asks for own-squares at every level just above `s`. (T3) asks only for own-squares on a
non-null set of levels in each interval `(s, s + ε)`. (T2) splits the claim into two halves of different
character:
- **eventual total collapse:** some level `M` above which all Bernoulli shifts are isomorphic;
- **threshold descent:** nontrivial isomorphisms whose lower base entropy approaches `s`.

## 5. The two halves are formally independent

Call an equivalence relation `≈` on `(0, ∞)` **formally admissible at level `s`** when it has four properties:
- it is analytic;
- it satisfies (F1);
- every non-rigid level is `≥ s`;
- `X_∞` is a class of its own.

For such a relation, define `D`, `W_d`, `S` and `R` by the formulas of Section 0. Then Items 1–4 and 6 of
`bernoulli-isomorphism-collapse-set-is-a-subgroup` hold for it, because their proofs use only (F1), transitivity
and the threshold. Item 5 is vacuous, because no finite level is `≈ ∞`.

**Model A (threshold descent without eventual collapse).** Let `Γ ⊂ R` be a countable dense subgroup. Put
`a ≈ b` iff `a = b`, or `a, b > s` and `b − a ∈ Γ`.
- It is Borel and satisfies (F1).
- `R = (s, ∞)` and `D = Γ`.
- For Item 6, `θ = s + 1` works for any finitely many elements of `Γ`.
- So `r = s`, but `D ≠ R`, and every class is countable.

**Model B (eventual collapse without threshold descent).** Fix `M > s`. Put `a ≈ b` iff `a = b` or
`a, b ≥ M`.
- It is closed and satisfies (F1).
- `D = R`, `R = [M, ∞)` and `r = M > s`.

So neither half of (T2) follows from the other by the product calculus of Items 1–4 and 6 together with Seward's
threshold. (Items 7 and 8 concern other groups and are not modelled.) These are models of the formal calculus, not group actions, and no group is known to realize either.

**Countable seeds give a null relation.**
- Let `P ⊂ (0, ∞)^2` be countable, and let `≈_P` be the smallest equivalence relation containing `P` and
  satisfying (F1).
- Let `Γ_P` be the subgroup generated by `{b − a : (a, b) ∈ P}`. The relation `b − a ∈ Γ_P` is an equivalence
  relation containing `P` and satisfying (F1). So `≈_P` is contained in it.
- Hence `≈_P` is planar-null, and each of its classes is countable.
- Any argument that obtains isomorphisms only by composing, inverting and multiplying by Bernoulli factors a
  countable list of seed isomorphisms therefore stays in case (N) of Theorem 2. It can give neither (v) nor (T1).
- Examples of such seeds are finitary codes between specific finite alphabets, or `2^G ≅ 3^G`.
- A proof of (T1) must produce uncountably many isomorphisms that the product calculus does not relate, for
  instance a family `h ↦ φ_h` over an interval of levels, or it must use a closure principle for `~`.

## 6. Remarks and limits

- **Closure principle.** Suppose `~` is relatively closed in `(0, ∞)^2`, and `D(G)` contains two rationally
  independent elements `d_1, d_2`. Then (v) holds.
  - Item 6 gives `θ` with `h ~ h'` whenever `h, h' ≥ θ` and `h' − h ∈ Z d_1 + Z d_2`.
  - This subgroup is dense, and `~` is closed, so `[θ, ∞)^2 ⊆ ~`.
  - If `D(G)` is closed, then `D(G) ∈ {0} ∪ {cZ : c > 0} ∪ {R}`, and `D(G) = R` iff (v).
  - The natural source of such closure would be finite determination of Bernoulli measures. On Thompson's `V`
    that fails at supremum zero (`thompson-v-bernoulli-finitely-determined-iff-pos`).
- **Zero supremum.** At `s = 0`, (T1) says that all `X_h` with `0 < h < ∞` are isomorphic. This is the
  finite-level part of the strong form recorded on `zero-rokhlin-supremum-forces-bernoulli-collapse`, which also
  asks for `X_h ≅ X_∞`. It implies that claim and is implied by its strong form. By Theorem 4 it is equivalent
  to own-squares on a non-null set of levels in every interval `(0, ε)`.
- **Positive finite supremum.** If `0 < s < ∞`, any of (T1)–(T4) would give `D(G) ≠ {0}` with `s > 0`. That answers
  the question "does `D(G) ≠ {0}` force `s = 0`?" on
  `bernoulli-isomorphism-collapse-set-is-a-subgroup` negatively. No contradiction with (T1) at `0 < s < ∞` is known.
- **What is not proved.**
  - No isomorphism between Bernoulli shifts of different base entropy is produced over any group.
  - Case (N) with `D(G) ≠ {0}`, for example with a countable `D(G)`, is not excluded.
  - It is not decided whether `r(G)` belongs to `R(G)`.
