## 1. Definition (new paragraph between l.55 and l.57), +12 source lines

NEW (inserted after "...answer all three forms positively."):
```
A \emph{marked group} $(G,s)$ is a group $G$ with a generating tuple
$s=(s_1,\dots,s_m)$. Marked groups $(Q_k,q_k)$ converge to $(G,s)$ in the
space of marked groups~\cite{Grigorchuk,Champetier} if for each word $w$
in $m$ letters and their inverses, and all large $k$ depending on $w$,
$w(q_k)=1$ if and only if $w(s)=1$. We call $(G,s)$ an \emph{expander
limit} of the $(Q_k,q_k)$ if moreover the $Q_k$ are finite,
$|Q_k|\to\infty$, and their Cayley graphs with respect to the $q_k$ form a
family of expanders: there is $\varepsilon>0$ such that for all $k$, every
$C\subseteq Q_k$ with $|C|\le|Q_k|/2$ has at least $\varepsilon|C|$
neighbors outside $C$, the neighbors of $a$ being the $aq_{k,i}^{\pm1}$. A
group is an expander limit of groups from a class if some marking of it is
an expander limit of marked groups from that class.
```
Reason: the same notion was spelled out four times in three different wordings (Thm 1 l.65–67, Thm 2 l.118–120, Cor 3 l.300–302, proof l.393–395). One name makes each statement a single quotable sentence.

Meaning checks:
- The quantifier order ("for each w and all large k depending on w") is exactly what l.223–231 prove: trivial words become trivial for large k, and nontrivial words stay nontrivial for all but finitely many k.
- Expansion "for all k" holds because (T) of EL_3(F) covers every finite quotient (l.233–235). |Q_k|→∞ is N_k→∞ (l.233).
- q_k generates Q_k for every k, because φ_k(S) generates M_{N_k}(F_2).
- Notation: I used C, not A, because A is the alphabet in Theorem 1.

Bounded term check: one web search for "expander limit" together with "space of marked groups". No existing use of the term with another meaning showed up.

## 7. Questions as a numbered list, +4 lines

OLD: l.512–529, three paragraphs holding five questions.
NEW (every question and citation kept; each item carries a motivation sentence, and item 1 keeps its two existing ones):
```
\section{Questions}\label{sec:questions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item\label{q:fp} Is there an infinite finitely presented simple group
with property~\textup{(T)} that is sofic, or at least hyperlinear?
Infinite finitely presented simple Kazhdan groups
exist~\cite{CapraceRemy}, while $G_X$ is not finitely presented: a
finitely presented LEF group is residually
finite~\cite{Stepin,VershikGordon}, and $G_X$ is not. A sofic example
would not be LEF, and it would also answer Open problem~6.1 of Alekseev
and Thom~\cite{AlekseevThom}, which asks for finitely presented sofic
groups with property~\textup{(T)} that are not residually finite.
\item Is every sofic infinite simple Kazhdan group LEF? A sofic example
for Question~\ref{q:fp} would answer this negatively.
\item Is every finitely generated sofic group a subgroup of an infinite,
finitely generated, simple Kazhdan sofic group? Corollary~\ref{cor:lef}
gives this for LEF groups, with LEF hosts.
\item Does $G_X\cong G_Y$ imply that $X$ and $Y$ are flip conjugate, or at
least strongly orbit equivalent~\cite{GPS}? Does $L(G_X)\cong L(G_Y)$
imply $G_X\cong G_Y$? If $(X,T)$ is topologically conjugate to $(Y,T')$ or
to $(Y,T'^{-1})$, then $G_X\cong G_Y$, and for topological full groups the
converse holds~\cite{GPS99,BezuglyiMedynets}.
\end{enumerate}
```
The item 2 motivation follows from item 1 as printed: a sofic example there is infinite, simple, Kazhdan and finitely presented, and it is not residually finite, so it is not LEF.
