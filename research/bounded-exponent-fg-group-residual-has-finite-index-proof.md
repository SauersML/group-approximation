**1.** `G/K` is finitely generated and has exponent dividing `N`. It is
residually finite: if `g` is not in `K`, some finite-index normal `L` omits
`g`, and `L/K` is a finite-index normal subgroup of `G/K` omitting `gK`. By
`fg-rf-bounded-exponent-groups-are-finite`, `G/K` is finite, so `K` has
finite index.

**2.** Let `L <= K` have finite index in `K`. Then `L` has finite index in
`G`, so its normal core `C = ⋂_(g in G) g L g^(-1)` is a finite-index normal
subgroup of `G`. Hence `K <= C <= L`, and `L = K`. The abelianization of `K`
is finitely generated (step 1 makes `K` finitely generated) and has bounded
exponent, so it is finite. Then `[K,K]` has finite index in `K`, and by what
was just shown `[K,K] = K`.

**3.** A finite-index subgroup of a finitely presented group is finitely
presented (Reidemeister–Schreier).

**4.** `K` has finite index in the infinite group `G`, so it is infinite. It
is finitely generated by step 1. Consider the proper normal subgroups of `K`,
ordered by inclusion. The union of a chain of them is proper, since otherwise
the finitely many generators of `K` would lie in one member. By Zorn's lemma
there is a maximal normal subgroup `M`, and `K/M` is simple. If `K/M` were
finite, `M` would be a proper finite-index subgroup of `K`, contradicting
step 2. So `K/M` is an infinite simple group, and its exponent divides `N`. ∎

**The "if and only if" for Problem 1.11.** If `G` is a finitely presented
infinite torsion group of bounded exponent, step 3 shows `K` is a finitely
presented infinite group of bounded exponent with no proper finite-index
subgroup. The converse is trivial. The profinite completion of `G` is
`G/K`, which is finite.
