## The question

C. Reid, MathOverflow 339541, "A variant on the Higman-Thompson groups" (2019):
- **The group `D`.** Let `C = Z/dZ` and
  `D = ⟨a_c (c ∈ C), t | a_c^2 = t^d = 1, t a_c t^{-1} = a_{c+1}⟩`.
- **The tree.** `E = ⟨a_c⟩`, and `T` is its Cayley graph on the `a_c`, a tree. `E` acts by
  left translation and `t` by conjugation.
- **The group `G`.** `X` is the closure of the rays in the product of all pairs of
  complementary half-trees, and `G` is the topological full group of `D` on `X`.

For `d > 2` this is the Higman–Thompson group `G_{d−1,d}`. Reid asks about `d = 0`,
`C = Z`: has it been studied, and what are its basic properties? He notes that the
derived group is finitely generated and simple, and that "it's not clear to me how to
compute the abelianization of G".

## Answer (d = 0)

**Identification.** Here `D = ⟨a_0⟩ * ⟨t⟩ = Z/2 * Z`.
- `T` is the Bass–Serre tree of `D` with the valence-2 `A`-vertices suppressed.
- Reid's `X` is the space of `free-product-boundary-groupoids-carry-the-vertex-group-homology`
  with `k = 2` and `B = Z = ⟨t⟩`: ends of `T` together with its vertices.
- So `G = F = [[D ⋉ X]]`, and that node gives the following.

1. **Dynamics.** `D ⋉ X` is essentially principal, minimal, purely infinite and
   expansive on a Cantor set.
   - At every vertex the germ group is `Z`, generated by the parabolic germ of `t`.
   - Branches `H_c` accumulate at the vertex from both ends of `Z`, and `t` shifts
     `H_c → H_{c+1}`. So `t` contracts on one side and expands on the other.
   - This differs from Higman–Thompson groups, where every germ at a fixed point is a
     contraction or an expansion. The proof that `G` is not one of them is item 4.
2. **Homology.** `H_0 = Z`, generated by a half-tree, with `[1_X] = 2`. `H_1 = Z`,
   generated by the germ of `t`. `H_n = 0` for `n >= 2`.
   - The invariant `μ` gives the half-tree `1` and gives a vertex neighbourhood with `j`
     branches removed `2 − j`. In Reid's notation this is `μ(X) = 2`, `μ([a_c]) = 1`.
3. **Abelianization.** `H_2 = 0`, so Li's sequence is `0 → Z/2 → G_ab → Z → 0`. Hence
   `G_ab ≅ Z ⊕ Z/2`.
   - **The `Z` quotient.** Write `g ∈ G` as `d_i` on the pieces `U_i` of a clopen
     partition. Up to sign, the index map is

         η(g) = ½ Σ_i μ(U_i) · τ(d_i),

     where `τ : D → Z` is the `t`-exponent. It is an integer. The generator
     `[1_X ⊗ t]` of `H_1` maps to `2[t]` in `H_1(D; Z)` under `μ`, and that map is
     injective. `η(t) = 1`.
   - **The `Z/2`.** It is generated by the transposition of two disjoint half-trees,
     for instance `t` on `[a_1]` and `t^{-1}` on `[a_2]`. That transposition is not a
     product of commutators.
4. **The derived group.** `G' = D(G)` is simple and finitely generated, and equals
   Nekrashevych's `A(G)`. By Li's rational corollary it is rationally acyclic, and
   `H_1(G'; Z) = 0`.
   - `H_*(G; Q)` is `Q` in degrees 0 and 1 and vanishes above.
   - `G` is not isomorphic to any Higman–Thompson group `V_{n,r}` or Brin–Higman–Thompson
     group, since those have finite abelianization.
5. **Open.** Is `G` (equivalently `G'`) finitely presented? Li's Garside finiteness
   theorem does not apply, because the vertices of `T` are infinite emitters.
   - Near a vertex, `t` together with the finitary permutations of the branches is a copy
     of the Houghton group `H_2 = FSym(Z) ⋊ Z`, which is not finitely presented.
   - That is only a heuristic; finite presentation of `G` is open.

## Lesson for general BH

A parabolic point creates a signed Euler measure (`2 − j`) and a free summand in `H_1`.
The full group then has infinite abelianization, and its simple derived group has the
homology of the germ group shifted up. The general mechanism is recorded in the parent
node: infinite vertex groups survive as isotropy and as homology. For hosts it means that
full groups with parabolic germs are a genuinely different family from Thompson-like
ones. Their finite presentation is the open gate.

**The identification**, checked:
- **The group.** For `d = 0`, `a_c = t^c a_0 t^{−c}`, so `D = ⟨a_0⟩ * ⟨t⟩`.
- **The tree.** Cosets `eB` (`e ∈ E`) are the `B`-vertices. The `A`-vertex `e t^c A` joins
  `e t^c B = eB` to `e t^c a_0 B = e a_c B`, so Reid's Cayley graph is the Bass–Serre tree with the
  valence-2 `A`-vertices suppressed. `E` acts by left translation and `t` by conjugation, as Reid says.
- **The space.** A point of `P` in the closure of the rays is a consistent choice of half-trees in which
  each vertex has at most one edge pointing away from it. Following those edges gives either an end, or
  a vertex at which every edge points inward. Every vertex occurs, since it has infinite valence. So `X`
  is the ends together with the vertices, the parent's space with `k = 2` and `B = Z`.
