Write `s_0, s_1, t_0, t_1` for the generators, with `t_i s_j = δ_ij` and
`s_0 t_0 + s_1 t_1 = 1`.

**Normal-form basis.** Orient the relations as the reduction system
`t_i s_j -> δ_ij` and `s_1 t_1 -> 1 - s_0 t_0`, with integer coefficients.
- **Overlaps.** The overlaps are `t_i s_1 t_1` and `s_1 t_1 s_j`. For `t_i s_1 t_1`,
  one reduction gives `δ_i1 t_1` and the other `t_i - δ_i0 t_0`; these agree for
  `i = 0, 1`. For `s_1 t_1 s_j`, one gives `s_j - δ_j0 s_0` and the other `δ_j1 s_1`;
  these agree for `j = 0, 1`.
- **Consequence.** By Bergman's diamond lemma, which holds over any commutative base
  ring, the irreducible words (no subword `t_i s_j`, no subword `s_1 t_1`) form a basis
  of `L_A(1,2)` over every commutative ring `A`.
- **Base change.** For `A ⊆ k`, `L_A(1,2)` is the `A`-span of these words inside
  `L_k(1,2)`. It is a subring, because every reduction has integer coefficients.
- **Two basis words.** `1` and `z = s_0 t_1` are irreducible basis words. Also
  `z^2 = s_0 (t_1 s_0) t_1 = 0`.

**Part 1.** Finitely many elements of `R` involve finitely many coefficients in their
normal forms. Let `A` be the subring of `k` they generate. Then those elements, their
products and sums lie in `L_A`, and so does any subgroup of `U` they generate together
with their inverses. For `k = Q`, a subring generated by finitely many rationals is
`Z[1/m]`, where `m` is the product of their denominators.

**Part 2.** A finitely generated ring that is an infinite field does not exist: a field
that is a finitely generated `Z`-algebra is finite, by the Nullstellensatz over `Z`. So
the ring `A` of part 1 is a proper subring of `k`.
- **U is not finitely generated.** Suppose it were, with `A` as above for a generating
  set and its inverses. Then `U ⊆ L_A`. A scalar `λ ∈ k^x` lies in `L_A` only if
  `λ ∈ A`, by its coefficient on the basis word `1`. So `k^x ⊆ A` and `k = A`, a
  contradiction.
- **U/k^x is not finitely generated.** Suppose it were, with finitely many generators
  lifted to `U`, and `A` built from these lifts and their inverses. Then
  `U = ⟨lifts⟩ · k^x` with `⟨lifts⟩ ⊆ L_A^x`. Pick `λ ∈ k \ A` and set
  `u = 1 + λ z`, a unit with inverse `1 - λ z`. Write `u = g μ` with `g ∈ L_A^x` and
  `μ ∈ k^x`. Then:
  - `g = μ^-1 + μ^-1 λ z ∈ L_A` gives `μ^-1 ∈ A` and `μ^-1 λ ∈ A`, from its
    coefficients on `1` and `z`;
  - `g^-1 = μ - μ λ z ∈ L_A` gives `μ ∈ A`.

  So `λ = μ · (μ^-1 λ) ∈ A`, a contradiction.

**Part 3.** Let `S ⊆ R` be a finitely generated subring, with `S ⊆ L_A` as in part 1,
where `A` is a finitely generated domain of characteristic zero.
1. **A prime that is not a unit.** By generic freeness, some `N >= 1` makes
   `A[1/N]` free over `Z[1/N]`, and `A[1/N] ≠ 0`. So for a prime `p ∤ N`,
   `A[1/N]/p ≠ 0`, and `p` is not a unit of `A`.
2. **Reduction.** Choose a maximal ideal `m ⊇ pA`. `F = A/m` is a finite field of
   characteristic `p`. Reduction of coefficients is a unital ring homomorphism
   `L_A -> L_F`. `L_F ≠ 0`, since the word `1` is a basis element. It sends `p·1`
   to `0`.
3. **Conclusion.** So `p·1` is not a unit of `L_A`. Hence `S` contains no inverse of
   `p`, and `Q ⊄ S`.

**`L_k(1,n)`.** The reduction system `t_i s_j -> δ_ij` and
`s_(n-1) t_(n-1) -> 1 - Σ_(i<n-1) s_i t_i` is confluent by the same two overlap
checks. So parts 1–3 go through verbatim.

QED
