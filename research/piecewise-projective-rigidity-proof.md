Lane proof, elementary, not independently reviewed. Notation as in the target.

## Step 0: the parameter variety

Suppose `G` is finitely presented. Since finite presentability does not depend on the finite
generating set, we may use a finite generating set `X` that contains generators `h_1, …, h_m` of
`H` (with algebraic data) and the element `u`. Concatenate the data of all `y ∈ X` into one vector
`x_0 ∈ R^N`. Let `V ⊆ A^N` be its Zariski closure over `Q`, i.e. the zero set of all
`f ∈ Q[x_1..x_N]` with `f(x_0) = 0`. It is `Q`-irreducible. For `x ∈ V(R)`, let `y(x)` be the map
with the data of `y` replaced by the corresponding coordinates of `x`.

## Step 1: genericity lemma

Let `f` be a rational function over `Q` that is regular at `x_0`.

- (a) If `f(x_0) = 0` then `f = 0` on `V`. This is the definition of `V`.
- (b) If `f(x_0)` is algebraic, then `f` is constant near `x_0` on `V(R)`. Indeed, if `m` is its
  minimal polynomial, then `m(f) = 0` on `V` by (a). So `f` takes finitely many values, and
  `V(R)` is locally connected because it is semialgebraic.
- (c) If `f(x_0)` is transcendental, then `f` takes values both above and below `f(x_0)` at points
  of `V(R)` arbitrarily close to `x_0`. The argument has three steps.
  - The singular locus of `V` is a proper closed subset defined over `Q`, so `x_0` is a smooth point
    and `V(R)` is a manifold of dimension `d = dim V` near `x_0`.
  - The locus in the smooth part where `d(f|_V) = 0` is closed and defined over `Q`. If it
    contained `x_0`, it would contain the whole smooth part, and `f` would be constant, equal to
    `f(x_0)`, on a connected manifold neighborhood `M` of `x_0`.
  - The complex Zariski closure of `M` has dimension `d`, so it contains a geometric component
    `V'` of `V`. Every such component has dimension `d`, being a Galois conjugate. So `f` would be
    constant on `V'`. But `V'` has `Q̄`-points in the domain of `f`, so `f(x_0) ∈ Q̄`, a
    contradiction. Hence `d(f|_V)(x_0) ≠ 0`, and `f` is a submersion at `x_0`.

## Step 2: deformation lemma

**Claim.** For every word `w` in `X^{±1}` there is a neighborhood `U_w` of `x_0` in `V(R)` such
that, for `x ∈ U_w`, the composite `w(x)` is a piecewise projective homeomorphism. Its
breakpoints and piece matrices are fixed rational functions of `x`, regular on `U_w`, which at
`x_0` give `w`. Moreover `w(x_0) = id` implies `w(x) = id` on `U_w`.

*Generators.* The conditions for `y(x)` to be a piecewise projective homeomorphism are of two kinds.
- *Identities:* continuity at the breakpoints, `y` fixing a finite endpoint of `I`, and `γ = 0` on
  unbounded pieces. These hold at `x_0`, hence on `V` by (a).
- *Strict inequalities:* breakpoints increasing, `αδ - βγ > 0`, and `γt + δ` of one sign at both
  ends of each bounded piece. These hold at `x_0`, hence near `x_0`.

Inverses are given by adjugate matrices and the images of breakpoints, both rational in `x`.

*Composites.* Induct on length, as in `pl-transcendental-endpoint-non-fp-proof`.
- For `y ∘ w'`, the candidate breakpoints are those of `w'` and the preimages `Q^{-1}(c)` of the
  breakpoints `c` of `y` under the piece matrices `Q` of `w'`. Both are rational in `x`.
- The combinatorics (which candidate lies in which piece, and their order) is decided by the signs
  of finitely many differences. By (a), each difference is either `0` on `V` or nonzero at `x_0`,
  hence of constant sign near `x_0`.
- If `w(x_0) = id`, every piece matrix `M` is scalar at `x_0`: `M_12 = M_21 = M_11 - M_22 = 0`.
  These identities then hold on `V`, so `w(x) = id`.

## Step 3: epimorphisms onto nearby groups

Let `G = ⟨X | r_1..r_k⟩`, and let `U` be the intersection of the `U_{r_j}` and the `U_y` for
`y ∈ X`. For `x ∈ U` each `r_j(x) = id`, so by von Dyck `y ↦ y(x)` extends to a homomorphism
`φ_x` with `φ_x(w(x_0)) = w(x)` for every word `w`. In the language of marked groups: the marked
group generated by the `y(x)` is a marked quotient of `G`.

## Step 4: the algebraic subgroup does not move

The coordinates of `x_0` that belong to the `h_i` are algebraic. By (b) they are constant near
`x_0`, so `h_i(x) = h_i` as maps. Shrinking `U`, we get `φ_x(h) = h` for every `h ∈ H` and every
`x ∈ U`, because `h` is a word in the `h_i^{±1}`.

## Step 5: the transcendental endpoint moves

- *`e` is a breakpoint of `u`.* The piece of `u` containing points just left of `e` has a
  non-scalar matrix `P`, since `u` moves them. If that piece contained `[e, e+η)` for some `η > 0`,
  then `P` would fix an interval and be scalar. So `e` is the right end of that piece `[c, e]`,
  and `e = e(x_0)`, `c = c(x_0)` are coordinates of `x_0`.
- *Choosing the deformation.* By (c), pick `x ∈ U` with `e(x) > e` and with `c(x) < e` and
  `P(x)` still non-scalar. The last two are open conditions.
- *Choosing the bump.* `P(x)` has at most two fixed points. `L` accumulates at `e` from the right,
  so pick `r ∈ L` with `e < r < min(e(x), e+ε)` and `P(x)(r) ≠ r`. Then pick `h ∈ H` with
  `inf supp h = r` and `supp h ⊆ (e, e+ε)`.
- *The contradiction.* At `x_0`, `supp u` misses `[e, e+ε) ⊇ supp h`, so `[u, h] = 1` in `G`.
  Applying `φ_x` gives `u(x) h u(x)^{-1} = h`, so `u(x)(supp h) = supp h`. Taking infima gives
  `u(x)(r) = r`. But `c(x) < r < e(x)`, so `u(x)(r) = P(x)(r) ≠ r`.

So `G` is not finitely presented whenever `e` is transcendental. ∎

The mirror case is the same with suprema. For `RP^1`, run Steps 0--4 with arcs and cyclic order,
and Step 5 in an affine chart around `e`.

## Corollary checks

- *Dyadic `F` on `D`.* For dyadic `r < s` in `D` there is `k ∈ F` with support exactly `(r,s)`,
  a standard fact. So `L` accumulates at each interior point of `D`.
- *`⟨F, F^λ⟩`.* Take `u` a bump of `F^λ` with support `(0, λq)`, and `e = λq ∈ (0,1)`, which is
  transcendental.
- *Lodha--Moore.* This relies on the stated density of support endpoints of `⟨a,b⟩`, from
  arXiv:1308.4250; not re-read.
