**Cited inputs.**

- (Far1) D. Farley, *Finiteness and CAT(0) properties of diagram groups*, Topology 42 (2003) 1065–1082.
  The cube complex of the diagram group `F = D(⟨x | x = x²⟩, x)` is CAT(0), and `F` acts on it properly.
- (Far2) D. Farley, *Proper isometric actions of Thompson's groups on Hilbert space*, IMRN 2003, no. 45,
  2409–2414. Same statement for `V`.
- Roller / Guralnik / Nevo–Sageev: in a CAT(0) cube complex each hyperplane `H` separates `X⁽⁰⁾` into exactly
  two halfspaces. The Roller compactification `X̄` is the set of ultrafilters: choices of one halfspace per
  hyperplane, pairwise intersecting. A vertex `x` gives the principal ultrafilter `σ_x = {halfspaces ∋ x}`,
  and `∂X = X̄ ∖ {σ_x}`. None of this needs finite dimension.
- Moore's tower, used only in item 4.

## Step 1. Upper halfspaces are well defined

- By (G1), each edge `e` has a lower endpoint `e⁻` and an upper endpoint `e⁺`, with `h(e⁺) = h(e⁻) + 1`.
- Take a square `Q`. By (G1) its vertices are `b, b_1, b_2, t` with heights `n, n+1, n+1, n+2`. Its two pairs of
  opposite edges are `{(b,b_1), (b_2,t)}` and `{(b,b_2), (b_1,t)}`.
  - The lower endpoints of the first pair are `b` and `b_2`. They are joined by the edge `(b,b_2)`, which is
    dual to the other hyperplane of `Q`.
  - Hyperplanes of a CAT(0) cube complex do not self-intersect, so that hyperplane is not `H`, and `b, b_2` lie
    on the same side of `H`.
  - The second pair is symmetric.
- The edges dual to `H` form the equivalence class generated by "opposite in a square". So all lower
  endpoints of edges dual to `H` lie on one side `H⁻`, and all upper endpoints lie on the other side `H⁺`.

## Step 2. Ascending paths cross each hyperplane once, upward

- An ascending edge path crosses `H` only along an edge dual to `H`, and it traverses that edge from `e⁻` to
  `e⁺`, that is, from `H⁻` to `H⁺`.
- Leaving `H⁺` would need a crossing from `H⁺` to `H⁻`, which means traversing a dual edge downward. So the
  path crosses `H` at most once.
- Hence for `x ≤ y`, the hyperplanes separating `x` and `y` are exactly those the path crosses, and each has
  `x ∈ H⁻`, `y ∈ H⁺`. In particular `d(x,y) = h(y) − h(x)`, and each `H⁺` is upward closed.

## Step 3. `σ_h = {H⁺}` is a non-principal ultrafilter

- *Pairwise intersecting.* Given `H, K`, pick `x ∈ H⁺` and `y ∈ K⁺` (upper endpoints of dual edges). By (G2)
  there is `ω ≥ x, y`. By Step 2, `ω ∈ H⁺ ∩ K⁺`.
- *Non-principal.* Take a vertex `x`. By (G3) it has an up-neighbour `x'`, and the edge `(x, x')` is dual to
  some `H` with `x ∈ H⁻`. So `H⁻ ∈ σ_x` while `H⁺ ∈ σ_h`, and `σ_h ≠ σ_x`. Therefore `σ_h ∈ ∂X`.

## Step 4. Invariance and the horofunction

- *Invariance.* An automorphism `g` with `h∘g = h` maps each dual edge of `H` to a dual edge of `gH` and
  preserves which endpoint is upper. So `g(H⁺) = (gH)⁺` and `gσ_h = σ_h`.
- *Horofunction.*
  - Set `c(x,y) = Σ_H (1_{H⁺}(y) − 1_{H⁺}(x))`. Only the finitely many hyperplanes separating `x` and `y`
    contribute, and `c` is additive: `c(x,y) + c(y,z) = c(x,z)`.
  - For `x ≤ ω`, Step 2 gives `c(x,ω) = h(ω) − h(x)`.
  - For arbitrary `x, y`, choose `ω ≥ x, y` and compute
    `c(x,y) = c(x,ω) − c(y,ω) = h(y) − h(x)`.

## Step 5. Instance F

**The complex.**
- A vertex is a dyadic PL homeomorphism `φ : [0,1] → [0,n]`: finitely many breakpoints in `Z[1/2]`, slopes in
  `2^Z`. Its charts are `ψ_i(t) = φ⁻¹(t + i − 1)`, which map `[0,1]` onto the pieces `I_i = φ⁻¹[i−1,i]`.
- The expansion `ε_{n,i} : [0,n] → [0,n+1]` is the identity on `[0,i−1]`, has slope 2 on `[i−1,i]`, and is
  `t ↦ t+1` on `[i,n]`. It replaces the chart `ψ_i` by the two charts `ψ_i(t/2)` and `ψ_i((t+1)/2)`.
- A cube `[φ, S]`, for `S ⊆ {1,…,n}`, has vertex set `{ε_{S'}∘φ : S' ⊆ S}`, where `ε_{S'}` expands the intervals
  of `S'` simultaneously. These expansions commute.
- `F` acts by `g·φ = φ∘g⁻¹`. Since `ε∘(φ∘g⁻¹) = (ε∘φ)∘g⁻¹`, the action maps cubes to cubes and preserves
  `h(φ) = n`.
- This is Farley's complex for the diagram group `D(⟨x | x=x²⟩, x) = F`. Reduced `(x, xⁿ)`-diagrams are exactly
  such `φ`, and adding one cell `x → x²` to the bottom is `ε_{n,i}`. It is CAT(0) by (Far1).

**The hypotheses.**
- (G1) `[φ, S]` has lowest vertex `φ`, and `h(ε_{S'}φ) = n + |S'| = n + d(φ, ε_{S'}φ)`.
- (G3) `ε_{n,1}∘φ` is an up-neighbour of `φ`.
- (G2) Call `φ` *standard* if every chart is the increasing affine map onto a standard dyadic interval
  `[k2^{−m}, (k+1)2^{−m}]`. Two steps show directedness.
  - (a) Every vertex lies below a standard one. Each chart `ψ_i` is a dyadic PL homeomorphism between intervals
    with dyadic endpoints.
    - Choose a standard dyadic subdivision of `[0,1]` fine enough that `ψ_i` is affine on each piece `P`.
    - Refine it further, so that `ψ_i(P)` has length `2^{−m'}` and a dyadic left endpoint divisible by
      `2^{−m'}`. This is possible because halving `P` halves the image length, while the left endpoint stays
      in a fixed `2^{−M}Z`.
    - Then `ψ_i(P)` is a standard dyadic interval. Performing the expansions of the corresponding binary tree
      at interval `i`, for each `i`, gives a standard vertex above `φ`.
  - (b) Standard vertices form a directed set. A standard vertex is the same thing as a standard dyadic
    partition of `[0,1]`, and expanding interval `j` halves the `j`-th piece. Two standard partitions have a
    common standard refinement, which is reached from each by expansions.
- So the Grading Lemma applies, and `F` fixes `σ_F ∈ ∂X_F`.

**Simple transitivity on horospheres.** Take `φ, φ'` of height `n`.
- `g = φ'⁻¹∘φ : [0,1] → [0,1]` is dyadic PL, so `g ∈ F`, and `g·φ = φ∘g⁻¹ = φ'`.
- If `φ∘g⁻¹ = φ`, then `g = 1`.

So `{h = n}` is a free transitive `F`-set, and by Step 4 these level sets are exactly the horospheres of `σ_F`.

## Step 6. Instance V

**The complex.** Vertices are finite sets of charts `{φ_1,…,φ_n}`. Each `φ_i : C → C` is a prefix-replacement
homeomorphism onto a clopen piece, and the pieces partition `C`. The height is `h = n`, and an expansion replaces
`φ_i` by `φ_i∘s_0, φ_i∘s_1`. Cubes expand several distinct pieces, `V` acts by post-composition, and the complex is
CAT(0) by (Far2).

**The hypotheses.**
- (G1) and (G3) hold as for `F`.
- (G2):
  - Each `φ_i` is a prefix replacement `wu ↦ w'u` on the cones of a finite cone partition of `C`.
  - Expanding along that tree turns the charts into standard cone charts `s_{w'}`.
  - Two standard vertices, that is, two cone partitions, have a common cone refinement.

**The action on horospheres.**
- *Transitive.* Given vertices `{φ_i}` and `{φ'_i}` of height `n`, define `g` to be `φ'_i∘φ_i⁻¹` on piece `i`.
  Then `g ∈ V` and it carries one vertex to the other.
- *Stabilizers.* `g{φ_i} = {φ_i}` means that `g` permutes the charts, and such `g` is determined by the
  permutation. So the stabilizer is `S_n`.
- *Non-amenable.* `V ⊇ T ⊇ PSL_2(Z) ⊇ F_2`.

## Step 7. Items 3 and 4 of the claim

- Suppose `Z` is compact, `σ ∈ Z` is `F`-fixed, and the maps `m_k : Z → Prob(F)` satisfy
  `sup_z ‖g·m_k(z) − m_k(gz)‖₁ → 0` for each `g`.
- Then `ν_k = m_k(σ)` satisfies `‖gν_k − ν_k‖₁ → 0`, which is Reiter's condition, so `F` is amenable.
- The converse holds because every action of an amenable group is amenable.
- For `(Z, δ_σ)` with Zimmer amenability, the same evaluation applies to an approximately invariant net of
  measurable maps. The measure `δ_σ` is invariant, and its only point is `σ`.
- Item 4 is this evaluation with its support radius and defect recorded. Then apply Namioka's level-set
  lemma: for `ν ≥ 0`, `‖sν − ν‖₁ = ∫_0^∞ |s{ν>t} △ {ν>t}| dt` and `∫_0^∞ |{ν>t}| dt = 1`. So some level set
  `A` has `Σ_s |sA △ A| ≤ (Σ_s ‖sν − ν‖₁)|A|`. Finally apply Moore's theorem.

## What this route does not prove

- It does not decide whether `X_F` itself has property A as a metric space. If it did, `F` would be exact, via
  the coarse embedding given by the orbit map of the proper action.
- It says nothing about other compact `F`-spaces that have no invariant measure.
- The non-amenability item 5 records only that the hypotheses of the named theorems fail. It is not a theorem
  that those tools could never be adapted.
