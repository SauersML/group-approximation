Let `k` be a field, `G` a group, `H <= G` a subgroup for which `k[H]` is
directly finite — for instance any residually finite `H`, or any sofic one.
Let `f in k[H]` be an idempotent and let `alpha in f k[H] f`.

**Claim.**  If some `beta in k[G]` satisfies `alpha beta = f` or
`beta alpha = f`, then `alpha` is already invertible in the corner
`f k[H] f`: there is `b in f k[H] f` with `alpha b = b alpha = f`.

Two consequences in the form they are used.

1. Taking `f = 1`: an element of `k[H]` with a one-sided inverse anywhere in
   `k[G]` has a two-sided inverse in `k[H]`.
2. If `rho : k[G] -> S` is any ring map, then `rho(alpha)` is invertible in
   the corner `rho(f) S rho(f)`.  With `S = R` and `rho = pi` the evaluation
   map, and `pi(f) = 1`, this says `pi(alpha) in R^x`.

**Why it is a lemma worth naming.**  Searches for a Kaplansky counterexample
in `F_2[G]` naturally build candidates out of a short explicit list of group
elements.  This says the list is the whole problem: if it generates a subgroup
with directly finite group algebra, no partner drawn from anywhere in `G`
can help, and the candidate is dead before any support bound or machine search
is attempted.  Support size is the wrong measurement; the subgroup generated
by the support is the right one.

## Scope

The hypothesis is on the subgroup generated by the support of `alpha` — not
of `beta`, which is unconstrained and is exactly what the coset projection
discards.  It gives no information when that subgroup is one whose group
algebra is not known to be directly finite, which for a nonsofic `G` is the
generic case and is where the open problem actually lives.
