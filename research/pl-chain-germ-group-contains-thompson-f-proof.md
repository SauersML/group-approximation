Notation is that of the target node. Germs at `+∞` on the Cantorized line are the germs of the real
maps on `[N, ∞)`, as in the source node. Two homeomorphisms that agree on `[N, ∞)` agree on its
Cantorized neighbourhood, because doubled points are determined by one-sided limits.

## Step 1: square-map coordinates

Write `y = n + t` with `n = ⌊y⌋` and `t = {y}`. Then `Λ(y) = 2^n (1+t)`.

- **δ.** `Λ(y+1) = 2 Λ(y)`, so `Λ^-1 δ Λ = T_1`.
- **a.** For `t < 1/2`: `Λ(y) ∈ [2^n, 3·2^(n-1))` and `a(Λ(y)) = 2^(n+1)·2^n(1+t) - 4^n
  = 4^n(1 + 2t) = Λ(2y)`. For `t >= 1/2`: `a(Λ(y)) = 2^(n+2)·2^n(1+t) - 4^(n+1)
  = 2^(2n+1)(1 + (2t-1)) = Λ(2n+1+(2t-1)) = Λ(2y)`. So `Λ^-1 a Λ = D_2` on all of `R`. (The
  formulas for `a` on `[2^n, 2^(n+1))` follow from `a(2x) = 4 a(x)`.)
- **ρ.** `Λ^-1 ρ Λ = D_2^-1 T_1 D_2 = T_(1/2)`.
- **σ.** `Λ(y) + 1 = 2^n(1 + t + 2^-n)`. If `t + 2^-n < 1` this equals `Λ(y + 2^-n)`. Otherwise it equals
  `2^(n+1)(1 + (t + 2^-n - 1)/2)`, whose fractional part lies in `[0, 2^(-n-1))`, and this is
  `Λ(n + 1 + (t + 2^-n - 1)/2)`. This gives the formula for `e_0` (`n >= 0`).
- **Germs.** `Λ` maps neighbourhoods of `+∞` to neighbourhoods of `+∞`, so `Q` is the germ group of
  `⟨e_0, T_1, D_2⟩` at `+∞`. This proves item 1.

## Step 2: the splitting

- **Every germ has an asymptotic affine part.** Say `h = A + o(1)` if `h(y) - A(y) -> 0`. Every `g ∈ Q`
  satisfies `g = A_g + o(1)` for a unique `A_g ∈ ⟨T_1, D_2⟩`. Induct on word length, multiplying on the
  left:
  - for affine `A'`, `A'(A + o(1)) = A'A + o(1)`, since `A'` is Lipschitz;
  - `e_0^±1(z) = z + o(1)` as `z -> ∞` (its displacement is at most `2^(1-n)` on `[n, n+1)`), and
    `A(y) + o(1) -> ∞`.

  Uniqueness holds because an affine map `A - A'` that tends to `0` at `+∞` is `0`.
- **The homomorphism.** `g ↦ A_g` is a homomorphism `Q -> ⟨T_1, D_2⟩ ≅ BS(1,2)`. It is split by
  `δ ↦ T_1` and `s ↦ D_2`, and it kills `σ`.
- **The kernel is M.** The kernel is `M`. `Q/⟨⟨σ⟩⟩` is generated by the images of `δ` and `s`, which
  satisfy the `BS(1,2)` relation. So `BS(1,2) -> Q/⟨⟨σ⟩⟩ -> BS(1,2)` is the identity, and
  `⟨⟨σ⟩⟩ = M`. This proves item 2.

## Step 3: exact bend-local pictures (item 3)

**Integer windows.** For an integer `m >= 2` put `φ_m(y) = 2^(m-1)(y - m)`.
- *e_0 is ε there.* On `Y_m = [m-1, m+1-2^-m)`, `φ_m e_0 = ε φ_m`:
  - On `[m-1, m-2^(1-m))`, where `w = φ_m(y) < -1`, `e_0` adds `2^(1-m)`, which is `w ↦ w+1`.
  - On `[m-2^(1-m), m)`, where `w ∈ [-1, 0)`, `e_0(y) = m + (y - m + 2^(1-m))/2`, which is
    `w ↦ (w+1)/2`.
  - On `[m, m+1-2^-m)`, where `w >= 0`, `e_0` adds `2^-m`, which is `w ↦ w + 1/2`.
- *e_1 is T_1 there.* `e_1(y) = e_0(y - 1/2) + 1/2`. For `y ∈ [m-1/2, m+1/2-2^(1-m))`, `e_0` adds
  `2^(1-m)` to `y - 1/2`. So `φ_m e_1 = T_1 φ_m` there.
- *Common range.* In `w`, both identities hold on `[-2^(m-2), 2^(m-2) - 1)`.

**Half-integer windows.** For `n >= 2` put `ψ_n(y) = 2^(n-1)(y - n - 1/2)`.
- *e_0 is T_(1/2) there.* On `[n, n+1-2^-n)`, `e_0` adds `2^-n`, which is `w ↦ w + 1/2`.
- *e_1 is ε there.* `ψ_n(y) = φ_n(y - 1/2)`, so the integer-window computation at `n`, applied to
  `u = y - 1/2 ∈ Y_n`, gives `ψ_n e_1 = ε ψ_n` for `y ∈ [n-1/2, n+3/2-2^-n)`.
- *Common range.* In `w`, both identities hold on `[-2^(n-2), 2^(n-2) - 1/2)`.

**Words act by their pictures.**
- *Picture words.* Let `W` be a word of length `L` in `σ^±1, e_1^±1`. Let `P_1(W)` and `P_2(W)` be the
  same word in `(ε, T_1)` and in `(T_(1/2), ε)`.
- *Displacement.* Each of `ε^±1, T_1^±1, T_(1/2)^±1` moves every `w` by at most `1`.
- *Inverses.* An inverse letter is also given by its picture on the common range shrunk by `1`.
- *Conclusion.* If `|φ_m(y)| <= 2^(m-2) - 2 - L`, every intermediate point of the orbit of `y` under the
  letters of `W` stays in the common range. So `φ_m W(y) = P_1(W) φ_m(y)`, and likewise
  `ψ_n W(y) = P_2(W) ψ_n(y)`.

**Coverage and faithfulness.** Every `y >= 2` lies within `1/4` of an integer `m` or of a half-integer
`n + 1/2`, so `|φ_m(y)| <= 2^(m-3)` or `|ψ_n(y)| <= 2^(n-3)`. For `m`, `n` large this is at most
`2^(m-2) - 2 - L` (respectively `2^(n-2) - 2 - L`).
- *If both pictures are trivial,* then `W` is the identity on some `[y_L, ∞)`.
- *If `P_1(W)` moves some `w*`,* then `W` moves `φ_m^-1(w*)` for every large `m`. The same holds for
  `P_2`.

So a word is trivial as a germ iff both pictures are trivial, and `W ↦ (P_1(W), P_2(W))` is an
isomorphism of the germ group `⟨σ, e_1⟩` onto `⟨(ε, T_(1/2)), (T_1, ε)⟩ ≤ PL(R)^2`. ∎

## Step 4: Thompson's F (item 4)

- **The pictures of f and g.** By Step 3 the pictures of the words are:
  - `C ↦ (k, T_(1/2) ε^-1)`, where `k = ε T_1^-1` is the identity on `w <= 0`, is `w/2` on `[0, 1]`,
    and is `w - 1/2` for `w >= 1`;
  - `D_n ↦ (T_n k T_-n, ε^n T_(1/2) ε^-1 ε^-n)`.

  The exact computation in `experiments/pl-chain-germ-thompson-f-2026-09-17/check.py` gives the two
  coordinates of `f = [D_2, D_3]` and `g = σ f σ^-1`. It uses `pl.py`: canonical PL maps with rational
  breakpoints and exact composition. The output is in `output.txt`.
  - Coordinate 1: `f_1` is the one-bump map on `[5/2, 7/2]` given by `t/2 + 5/4`, then `t - 1/4`, then
    `2t - 7/2`, with breaks at `3, 13/4`. `g_1 = T_(1/2) f_1 T_(-1/2)` is the same map on `[3, 4]`.
  - Coordinate 2: `f_2` is the one-bump map on `[3/4, 3/2]` given by `t/2 + 3/8`, then `2t - 3/2`, with
    break `5/4`. `g_2 = T_(1/2) f_2 T_(-1/2)` is the same map on `[5/4, 2]`.
- **The relations, by hand from these formulas.** In each coordinate `i`, `x_0 x_1^-1 = f_i`, and
  `x_0^-j x_1 x_0^j` is supported on `x_0^-j(supp g_i)`.
  - Coordinate 1: `f_1^-1(3) = 13/4` and `g_1^-1(13/4) = 7/2`, so `x_0^-1(3) = 7/2`. Hence
    `x_0^-1 x_1 x_0` and `x_0^-2 x_1 x_0^2` are supported in `[7/2, 4]`. Their supports meet
    `supp f_1 = [5/2, 7/2]` in at most one point, so they commute with `f_1`.
  - Coordinate 2: `f_2^-1(5/4) = 11/8` and `g_2^-1(11/8) = 3/2`, so the conjugates are supported in
    `[3/2, 2]`, and `supp f_2 = [3/4, 3/2]`. Again they commute.

  So both relators are trivial in `PL(R)^2`, hence trivial in `Q` by Step 3.
- **Non-commuting.** `[x_0, x_1] = [f g, g] = [f, g]`. Its first coordinate is non-trivial:
  `f_1 g_1 f_1^-1` is supported on `f_1([3, 4]) = [11/4, 4]`, which is not `supp g_1`.
- **Conclusion.** By Cannon–Floyd–Parry (Enseign. Math. 42 (1996), Thm 3.4 and Thm 4.3),
  `F = ⟨x_0, x_1 | [x_0x_1^-1, x_0^-1x_1x_0], [x_0x_1^-1, x_0^-2x_1x_0^2]⟩`, and every proper quotient of
  `F` is abelian. So `⟨x_0, x_1⟩ ≅ F`. ∎
- **Independent check on the real maps.** The script also evaluates the words in the actual `σ`, `δ`
  and `a` on 3472 exact sample points `x ∈ [2^m, 2^(m+1))`, `m = 10..13`. The word lengths are 2772 and
  4156 for the two relators.
  - Both relators fix every sample.
  - `[x_0, x_1]` moves 152 samples. Near `2^m` they have `x - 2^m` between `89/16` and `111/16`, and
    near `3·2^(m-1)` they have `x - 3·2^(m-1)` between `33/16` and `47/16`. These are the predicted sets
    `2·[11/4, 7/2]` and `2·[1, 3/2]`, read at the sampling mesh `1/16`.

## Item 5

- **Not soluble, not residually soluble.** `F` is not soluble, and `F'` is simple and non-abelian
  (CFP Thm 4.5). So no soluble group contains `F`, and neither does any residually soluble group.
- **Not elementary amenable.** `F` is not elementary amenable (CFP Thm 4.10, via Chou). The class is
  subgroup-closed.
- **M.** `x_0, x_1 ∈ M`, because they are words in `σ` and `e_1 = ρ σ ρ^-1`, and both of these lie in
  the normal subgroup `M = ⟨⟨σ⟩⟩`. ∎
