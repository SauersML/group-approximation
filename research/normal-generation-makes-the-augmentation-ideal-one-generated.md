Let `S` be a group and `t in S`, and let `I ⊆ Z[S]` be the two-sided ideal
generated by `1 - t`.  If `t` normally generates `S`, then

    1 - s  in  I     for every  s in S ,

so `I` is the augmentation ideal of `Z[S]`.

In particular, if `S` is simple then this holds for **every** `1 != t in S`.

## Consequence for the ring lane

Suppose an extension `L ⊇ Z[S]` makes `1 - t` full for one nontrivial `t` in
a simple `S`.  Then every `1 - s` with `1 != s in S` is full in `L` as well,
since each lies in the ideal generated by `1 - t` already inside `Z[S]`.

This is the exact ring counterpart of
`every-nontrivial-core-element-normally-generates-the-envelope`, and it has
the same shape of limitation: it says the core is uniformly full, and it says
nothing about elements outside the core.  The Chatterji--Kassabov
construction chooses one `1 - g` and makes it full; this claim is why nothing
is lost by that choice, and `one-sided-sandwich-leaves-an-ideal-missing-the-core`
is why nothing more is gained by it.

## Where the simplicity hypothesis enters

Only through "`t` normally generates `S`".  The identity itself is about an
arbitrary group and an arbitrary element, and its content is that
`K = {s : 1 - s in I}` is a *normal subgroup*.  Simplicity is then just the
cheapest way to know that every nontrivial `t` normally generates, which is
what makes the conclusion uniform in `t`.
