\title{Infinite simple Kazhdan groups that are limits of finite simple groups}
\author{\paperauthorname}
\date{}
\subjclass[2020]{Primary 20E32; Secondary 03D40, 05C48, 16S35, 20E26, 20F10, 22D55, 37B10, 46L10}
\keywords{Property (T), sofic groups, hyperlinear groups, locally embeddable into finite groups, simple groups, minimal subshifts, word problem, expanders}

\begin{abstract}
Infinite simple groups with property~\textup{(T)} can be limits of finite
simple groups. For every infinite minimal subshift $X$, the group
$G_X=\EL_3(\LC(X,\F_2)\rtimes\Z)$ is an infinite, finitely generated,
simple group with property~\textup{(T)} and a limit of finite simple groups
$\SL_{3N}(\F_2)$ whose Cayley graphs form expanders, so it is locally
embeddable into finite groups (LEF), sofic and hyperlinear. This answers
the question of Brown and Ozawa whether an infinite simple Kazhdan group
can be hyperlinear, and Pestov's sofic version of it.
A finitely generated group is LEF if and only if it is a subgroup of an
infinite, finitely generated, simple Kazhdan group that is a limit of
finite simple groups whose Cayley graphs form expanders, and the host can
be chosen with a word problem of the same Turing degree as that of the
group. One such host contains every recursively presented finitely
generated LEF group.
\end{abstract}
\maketitle

Can an infinite simple group with property~\textup{(T)} be hyperlinear?
Brown asked this in 2001 as a question about embeddings into unitary
groups of McDuff factors that embed in an ultrapower $\mathcal R^\omega$
of the hyperfinite $\mathrm{II}_1$ factor~\cite[\S11, Question~7]{Brown}.
Ozawa stated the hyperlinear form in 2003, noting that the full
$C^*$-algebra of such a group cannot have the local lifting
property~\cite[\S7]{Ozawa}, and Pestov asked the sofic form as Open
question~9.1~\cite{Pestov}. Thom constructed a finitely generated Kazhdan
group that is locally embeddable into finite groups but not residually
finite, and so lacks the factorization property~\cite{Thom}, but his
example is not simple. Pestov and Kwiatkowska wrote that a hope of
constructing groups that are not hyperlinear from property~\textup{(T)}
remained, since it was unknown whether finitely generated simple Kazhdan
groups could be hyperlinear~\cite{PestovKwiatkowska}. Arzhantseva listed
the sofic form among open questions, and gave an infinite simple Kazhdan
group that is asymptotically residually finite, a weaker property in which
the generating set may change with the radius~\cite[\S3]{Arzhantseva}. The
groups below answer all three forms of the question positively.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Let $X\subseteq A^{\Z}$ be an infinite minimal subshift over a finite
alphabet, with shift $T$, and let $\LC(X,\F_2)$ be the ring of locally
constant functions $X\to\F_2$. Then
\[
  G_X=\EL_3\bigl(\LC(X,\F_2)\rtimes_T\Z\bigr)
\]
is an infinite, finitely generated, simple group with Kazhdan's
property~\textup{(T)}, and an expander limit, in the sense defined below,
of finite simple groups $\SL_{3N}(\F_2)$. So $G_X$ is locally embeddable
into finite groups \textup{(LEF)}, sofic and hyperlinear.
\end{theorem}

In Brown's form, $G_X$ lies in the unitary group of the McDuff factor
$L(G_X)\mathbin{\bar\otimes}\mathcal R$, which embeds in $\mathcal R^\omega$
(Section~\ref{sec:proof}).

Here $X$ is a closed shift-invariant set in which every orbit is dense. A
\emph{marked group} is a group with a finite generating tuple. Marked
groups $(Q_k,\bar q_k)$ converge to a marked group $(G,\bar g)$ in the
space of marked groups~\cite{Grigorchuk,Champetier} if the tuples have the
same length $m$ and, for each word $w$ in $m$ letters and their inverses
and all large $k$ depending on $w$, we have $w(\bar q_k)=1$ if and only if
$w(\bar g)=1$. We call $(G,\bar g)$ an \emph{expander limit} of the
$(Q_k,\bar q_k)$ if moreover the $Q_k$ are finite, $|Q_k|\to\infty$, and
their Cayley graphs with respect to the $\bar q_k$ form a family of
expanders: there is $\varepsilon>0$ such that for every $k$, every set of
at most $|Q_k|/2$ elements of $Q_k$ has at least $\varepsilon$ times as
many neighbors outside it as it has elements, the neighbors of an element
being its products with the entries of $\bar q_k$ and their inverses. A
group is an expander limit of groups from a class if some marking of it is
an expander limit of marked groups from that class.

A group is LEF~\cite{VershikGordon} if each finite subset $\Phi$ maps
injectively into a finite group by a map that preserves every product $gh$
with $g,h,gh\in\Phi$. Limits of finite groups in the space of marked groups
are LEF. Sofic and hyperlinear groups are defined by approximately
multiplicative maps into finite symmetric groups with the normalized
Hamming distance, and into unitary groups with the normalized
Hilbert--Schmidt distance, that keep nontrivial elements uniformly far
from~$1$~\cite{Pestov}. Hyperlinear groups were named by
R\u{a}dulescu~\cite{Radulescu}, and sofic groups were introduced by
Gromov~\cite{Gromov99} and named by Weiss~\cite{Weiss}.

The proof has three ingredients.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Property~\textup{(T)}.} The ring $\LC(X,\F_2)\rtimes_T\Z$
consists of the finite sums $\sum_jf_ju^j$ with $f_j\in\LC(X,\F_2)$,
multiplied using $ufu^{-1}=f\circ T^{-1}$. It is finitely generated, so
$G_X$ has Kazhdan's property~\textup{(T)}~\cite{Kazhdan} by the theorem of
Ershov and Jaikin-Zapirain~\cite{EJZ}.
\item \emph{Simplicity.} A nontrivial normal subgroup contains a nontrivial
commutator lying in a copy of the finite simple group $\GL_d(\F_2)$ over a
clopen tower, a small clopen set with its disjoint translates
(Figure~\ref{fig:models}(b)). So it contains this group and with it an
elementary matrix, from which minimality gives every elementary matrix.
\item \emph{Finite models.} A periodic sequence of period $N$ approximating
$X$ turns the shift into a cyclic permutation matrix and each locally
constant function into a diagonal matrix (Figure~\ref{fig:models}(a)), as
in the proof by Grigorchuk and Medynets that topological full groups of
minimal Cantor systems are LEF~\cite[Theorem~2.6]{GM}. These models make
$G_X$ a limit of the groups $\SL_{3N}(\F_2)$ in the space of marked groups.
The $\SL_{3N}(\F_2)$ are quotients of one Kazhdan group, $\EL_3$ of a
finitely generated free algebra over $\F_2$, so their Cayley graphs form
expanders as in Kassabov's construction~\cite{Kassabov}, and $G_X$ is an
expander limit of them.
\end{itemize}

The same argument applies to other actions. An action on a Cantor set is
topologically free if no nontrivial element fixes a nonempty open set. We
write $e_{ij}(r)=I_n+rE_{ij}$, with $E_{ij}$ the matrix unit, for the
elementary matrices.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:general}
Let a finitely generated group $\Lambda$ act minimally and topologically
freely on a Cantor set $C$, and let the ring $R=\LC(C,\F_2)\rtimes\Lambda$
be generated by a finite set $S\ni1$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(a)}] For every $n\ge3$, the group $\EL_n(R)$ is an infinite,
finitely generated, simple Kazhdan group.
\item[\textup{(b)}] Suppose that there are maps
$\varphi_k\colon R\to M_{N_k}(\F_2)$ with $\varphi_k(1)=I$ such that
$\varphi_k(S)$ generates the ring $M_{N_k}(\F_2)$, and for all
$r,r'\in R$ and all large $k$, depending on $r$ and $r'$, we have
$\varphi_k(r+r')=\varphi_k(r)+\varphi_k(r')$ and
$\varphi_k(rr')=\varphi_k(r)\varphi_k(r')$. Then for every $n\ge3$,
$\EL_n(R)$, marked by the $e_{ij}(s)$ with $s\in S$, is an expander limit
of the finite simple groups $\SL_{nN_k}(\F_2)$ marked by the
$e_{ij}(\varphi_k(s))$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Periodic approximations of $X$ give such maps, and Theorem~\ref{thm:main}
follows. Lamplighter actions show that the countable subgroups of the
groups in Theorem~\ref{thm:general}\textup{(b)} are exactly the countable
LEF groups (Corollary~\ref{cor:host}). Over $\Z$ the subshift hypothesis
cannot be dropped (Section~\ref{sec:main}).

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:general}}\label{sec:proof}

We write the proof for $n=3$; for $n\ge3$ replace $3$ by $n$ throughout.

\subsection*{The ring and property (T)}
Write $e_U$ for the indicator of a clopen set $U\subseteq C$. The ring
$R=\bigoplus_{\xi\in\Lambda}\LC(C,\F_2)\,u_\xi$ has the multiplication
$(fu_\xi)(f'u_\eta)=f\,(f'\circ\xi^{-1})\,u_{\xi\eta}$, so
$u_\xi e_Uu_\xi^{-1}=e_{\xi U}$. Fix a finite symmetric generating set of
$\Lambda$, let $|\xi|$ be the word length, and put
$B_m=\{\xi\in\Lambda:|\xi|\le m\}$. Let $G=\EL_3(R)$ be the subgroup of
$\GL_3(R)$ generated by the $e_{ij}(r)$ with $i\ne j$ and $r\in R$. With
$[g,h]=ghg^{-1}h^{-1}$ and $i,j,l$ distinct,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:elementary}
 e_{ij}(r+r')=e_{ij}(r)e_{ij}(r'),\qquad [e_{il}(r),e_{lj}(r')]=e_{ij}(rr').
\end{equation}
So the $e_{ij}(s)$ with $s\in S$ generate $G$. By Ershov and
Jaikin-Zapirain~\cite[Theorem~1.1]{EJZ}, $\EL_n$ of every finitely
generated associative unital ring has property~\textup{(T)} for $n\ge3$,
so $G$ has property~\textup{(T)}. It is infinite because
$e_{12}(\LC(C,\F_2))$ is infinite.

\subsection*{Simplicity}
Let $1\ne K\trianglelefteq G$ and $1\ne g\in K$. We show in three steps
that $K=G$.

\subsubsection*{Step~1: a nontrivial commutator with a small elementary matrix}
Let $w\ge0$ bound the word lengths of the $\xi$ with $u_\xi$ occurring in
the entries of $g$ and $g^{-1}$. Call a clopen set $V$ small if (i)
$V\cap\xi V=\varnothing$ for every $\xi\in B_{2w}\setminus\{e\}$, and (ii)
$f\circ\xi$ is constant on $V$ for every $\xi\in B_w$ and every
coefficient $f\in\LC(C,\F_2)$ of an entry of $g$ or $g^{-1}$. Each
$\xi\ne e$ fixes a closed set with empty interior, so the points moved by
every $\xi\in B_{2w}\setminus\{e\}$ form a dense open set. Small enough
clopen neighborhoods of these points are small, so every nonempty clopen
set contains a nonempty small one.

Some $h=e_{ij}(e_V)$ with $V$ small does not commute with $g$. Indeed, $g$
commutes with $e_{ij}(r)$ exactly when $g_{pi}r=0$ for $p\ne i$,
$rg_{jq}=0$ for $q\ne j$, and $g_{ii}r=rg_{jj}$. For $c=\sum_\xi c_\xi u_\xi$
and small $V$ we have $ce_V=\sum_\xi c_\xi e_{\xi V}u_\xi$ and
$e_Vc=\sum_\xi e_Vc_\xi u_\xi$. If $c_\xi\ne0$, it equals $1$ on a nonempty
clopen set $U$, and a small set $V\subseteq\xi^{-1}U$ gives $ce_V\ne0$,
while a small set $V\subseteq U$ gives $e_Vc\ne0$. So if $g$ commutes with
every such $h$, then $g$ is diagonal. In $g_{ii}e_V=e_Vg_{jj}$ the
coefficients at $u_\xi$ with $\xi\ne e$ are supported in the disjoint sets
$\xi V$ and $V$, so they vanish, and the same argument shows that the
corresponding coefficients of $g_{ii}$ and $g_{jj}$ vanish. The
coefficients at $u_e$ agree on every small $V$, so they agree everywhere.
Therefore $g=cI_3$ with $c\in\LC(C,\F_2)$, and comparing coefficients at
$u_e$ in $cc^{-1}=1$ gives $c=1$ and $g=1$. Fix such $h$ and $V$. Then
$x=[g,h]$ lies in $K\setminus\{1\}$.

\subsubsection*{Step~2: the commutator lies in a finite simple group over a tower}
Put $d=3|B_w|$. For $a,b\in B_w$ put $\epsilon_{ab}=e_{aV}u_{ab^{-1}}$
(Figure~\ref{fig:models}(b)). Since
$\epsilon_{ab}\epsilon_{a'b'}=e_{a(V\cap b^{-1}a'V)}\,u_{ab^{-1}a'b'^{-1}}$
and $b^{-1}a'\in B_{2w}$, we get
$\epsilon_{ab}\epsilon_{a'b'}=\delta_{ba'}\epsilon_{ab'}$. As $h\ne I_3$,
$V$ is nonempty, so the $\epsilon_{ab}$ span a ring
$A_V\cong M_{|B_w|}(\F_2)$ with unit $1_V=\sum_a\epsilon_{aa}$, and
$y\mapsto(1-1_V)I_3+y$ embeds $\GL_3(A_V)\cong\GL_d(\F_2)$ in $\GL_3(R)$. Its
image $H_V$ lies in $G$, since the transvections $e_{pq}(\epsilon_{ab})$
with $p\ne q$ generate it: the other transvections of $\GL_d(\F_2)$ are
commutators of these.

If $a,b\in B_w$ and $f,f'$ are coefficients as above, then
\[
  fu_a\,e_V\,f'u_b=f\,e_{aV}\,(f'\circ a^{-1})\,u_{ab}
  \in\{0,\epsilon_{a,b^{-1}}\},
\]
since $f\circ a$ and $f'$ are constant on $V$. The entries of
$ghg^{-1}-I_3=g\,e_VE_{ij}\,g^{-1}$ are sums of such products, and
$h^{-1}=h=I_3+\epsilon_{ee}E_{ij}$, so $x-I_3=(ghg^{-1}-h)h$ and
$x^{-1}-I_3=h(ghg^{-1}-h)$ lie in $M_3(A_V)$. Then $y=1_VI_3+(x-I_3)$ and
$y'=1_VI_3+(x^{-1}-I_3)$ satisfy $yy'=y'y=1_VI_3$, so
$x=(1-1_V)I_3+y\in H_V$. As $K\cap H_V$ is a nontrivial normal subgroup of
$H_V\cong\GL_d(\F_2)=\PSL_d(\F_2)$, which is simple as $d\ge3$, we get
$H_V\subseteq K$, and $e_{pq}(e_V)\in K$ for all $p\ne q$.

\subsubsection*{Step~3: $K$ contains every elementary matrix}
The set $J=\{r\in R:e_{pq}(r)\in K\text{ for all }p\ne q\}$ is a
two-sided ideal by~\eqref{eq:elementary}, since
$e_{pq}(r'r)=[e_{pl}(r'),e_{lq}(r)]$ and
$e_{pq}(rr')=[e_{pl}(r),e_{lq}(r')]$ for $l\notin\{p,q\}$. It contains
$e_V$, so it contains every $e_{\xi V}=u_\xi e_Vu_\xi^{-1}$. By minimality
and compactness finitely many of these sets cover $C$, so
$1=1-\prod_i(1-e_{\xi_iV})\in J$. Then $J=R$ and $K=G$. This proves~(a).

For derived topological full groups, Matui showed by a similar argument
that a nontrivial normal subgroup meets a simple union of products of
alternating groups on towers~\cite[Lemma~3.4 and Theorem~4.9]{Matui}. For the ring
$\LC(X,\F_2)\rtimes_T\Z$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, simplicity also follows
from Stepanov's theorem on the normal structure of
$\GL_n$~\cite[Theorem~4.4]{Stepanov}: this ring is
simple~\cite[Corollary~4.6]{ClarkEdie}, and for any $p,q$ in it there are
$r,r'$, not both $0$, with $pr+qr'=0$, by a dimension count on a tower over
one small cylinder.

\subsection*{Finite simple models}
Let $\mathcal A$ be the free algebra over $\F_2$ on letters $\tau_s$,
$s\in S$, and let $\pi\colon\mathcal A\to R$ and
$\rho_k\colon\mathcal A\to M_{N_k}(\F_2)$ be the ring homomorphisms with
$\pi(\tau_s)=s$ and $\rho_k(\tau_s)=\varphi_k(s)$. Both are onto, so $G$
and $\EL_3(M_{N_k}(\F_2))$ are quotients of $\EL_3(\mathcal A)$. Here
$\EL_3(M_{N_k}(\F_2))=\SL_{3N_k}(\F_2)$, as transvections generate
$\SL_{3N_k}(\F_2)$, and each transvection inside one block is a commutator
of transvections between blocks. The group $\EL_3(\mathcal A)$ is
generated by the $e_{ij}(1)$ and $e_{ij}(\tau_s)$, and in both quotients
$e_{ij}(1)$ and $e_{ij}(\tau_s)$ with $s=1$ have the same image. A word of
length $\lambda$ or less in the $e_{ij}(\tau_s)$ has entries of degree at
most $\lambda$ in $\mathcal A$, and $\varphi_k\circ\pi=\rho_k$ on the
finitely many such entries for large $k$, where $\varphi_k(0)=0$ by
additivity. So a word that is trivial in $G$ is trivial in
$\SL_{3N_k}(\F_2)$ for large $k$. Conversely, let $w$ be a word that is
nontrivial in $G$. As $G$ is simple, $e_{12}(1)$ is a product of conjugates
of $w$ and $w^{-1}$ in $G$. This relation holds in $\SL_{3N_k}(\F_2)$ for
large $k$, where $e_{12}(1)$ maps to the transvection $e_{12}(I)\ne I$, so
$w$ is nontrivial in $\SL_{3N_k}(\F_2)$ for large $k$. Therefore these
marked finite simple groups converge to $G$, so
$G$ is LEF, and $N_k\to\infty$ because $G$ is infinite. Since
$\EL_3(\mathcal A)$ has property~\textup{(T)}~\cite[Theorem~1.1]{EJZ}, the
Cayley graphs of its finite quotients with respect to the images of its
generators form a family of expanders~\cite{Margulis,Kassabov}, so $G$ is
an expander limit of the $\SL_{3N_k}(\F_2)$. This proves~(b).\qed

\subsection*{Brown's formulation}
Let $G$ be a group as in Theorem~\ref{thm:general}\textup{(b)}. It is
LEF, so it is sofic~\cite[Example~4.5]{Pestov} and
hyperlinear~\cite[Theorem~2]{ElekSzabo}, and $L(G)$ embeds in
$\mathcal R^\omega$~\cite{Kirchberg,Radulescu},~\cite[Proposition~7.1]{Ozawa}.
As $G$ is infinite and simple, its nontrivial conjugacy classes are
infinite, so $L(G)$ is a $\mathrm{II}_1$ factor. Then $G$ lies in the
unitary group of the McDuff factor $L(G)\mathbin{\bar\otimes}\mathcal
R$~\cite{McDuff}, which embeds in $\mathcal
R^\omega\mathbin{\bar\otimes}\mathcal R\subseteq(\mathcal
R\mathbin{\bar\otimes}\mathcal R)^\omega\cong\mathcal R^\omega$, as in
Brown's question.

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}}\label{sec:main}

Let $\Z$ act on $X$ by powers of $T$, where $(Tx)_t=x_{t+1}$, and write
$u=u_1$, so $ufu^{-1}=f\circ T^{-1}$. Minimality and infiniteness imply
that $X$ has no isolated points and $T$ has no periodic points, so $X$ is
a Cantor set and the action is topologically free. The ring
$R=\LC(X,\F_2)\rtimes_T\Z$ is generated by
$S=\{1,u,u^{-1}\}\cup\{e_a:a\in A\}$, where $e_a=e_{\{x:x_0=a\}}$, since
products of the translates $u^je_au^{-j}$ are the indicators of the
cylinders, the sets of points with a given word at given coordinates,
which span $\LC(X,\F_2)$.

Fix $x\in X$ and $\ell\ge0$. By minimality the forward orbit of $x$ is
dense, so every word of $X$ occurs in $x_{[0,\infty)}$, and
$x_{[-\ell,\ell]}$ recurs at arbitrarily large positions. So there is $m$
with $x_{[m-\ell,m+\ell]}=x_{[-\ell,\ell]}$ such that every word of length
$2\ell+1$ of $X$ occurs in $x_{[0,m)}$. The $m$-periodic sequence $y_\ell$
that agrees with $x$ on $[0,m)$ then agrees with $x$ on $[-\ell,m+\ell]$.
Every window of length $2\ell+1$ of $y_\ell$ is a translate of one
centered in $[0,m)$, so $y_\ell$ has the same words of length $2\ell+1$ as
$X$. Let $N_\ell$ be its least period, and on $\F_2^{\Z/N_\ell\Z}$ let
$P\delta_t=\delta_{t+1}$. For $f$ depending only on coordinates in
$[-\ell,\ell]$ let $D_\ell(f)\delta_t=f(T^ty_\ell)\delta_t$, where
$f(T^ty_\ell)$ is the value of $f$ at any point of $X$ that agrees with
$T^ty_\ell$ on $[-\ell,\ell]$. Put
$\varphi_\ell(\sum_jf_ju^j)=\sum_jD_\ell(f_j)P^j$ when every $f_j$ is of
this kind, and $\varphi_\ell=0$ otherwise. Since
$PD_\ell(f)P^{-1}=D_\ell(f\circ T^{-1})$ whenever $f$ depends only on
coordinates in $[-\ell+1,\ell-1]$, for fixed $r,r'\in R$ the identities
$\varphi_\ell(r+r')=\varphi_\ell(r)+\varphi_\ell(r')$ and
$\varphi_\ell(rr')=\varphi_\ell(r)\varphi_\ell(r')$ hold for large $\ell$,
and $\varphi_\ell(1)=I$. The shifts $T^ty_\ell$, $0\le t<N_\ell$, are
distinct, so products of the $P^iD_\ell(e_a)P^{-i}$ give every diagonal
matrix unit, and with $P$ every matrix unit. Therefore $\varphi_\ell(S)$
generates $M_{N_\ell}(\F_2)$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:general} applies.\qed

\subsection*{Sharpness over $\Z$}
For a homeomorphism $T$ of a Cantor set $X$, the group
$\EL_3(\LC(X,\F_2)\rtimes_T\Z)$ is simple and Kazhdan if and only if
$(X,T)$ is conjugate to a minimal subshift. One direction is
Theorem~\ref{thm:main}. Conversely, a Kazhdan group is finitely
generated~\cite[Theorem~1.3.1]{BHV}, and the entries of finitely many
generators and their inverses generate the ring, as $e_{12}(r)$ has the
entry $r$. So the ring is generated by finitely many $\sum_jf_ju^j$, the
translates of these $f_j$ generate the clopen sets, and the itinerary map
to the atoms they generate is a conjugacy onto a subshift. If a nonempty
proper closed set $Y$ is invariant, restriction maps the group onto
$\EL_3(\LC(Y,\F_2)\rtimes_T\Z)$ with a kernel that contains $e_{12}(e_U)$
for a nonempty clopen set $U\subseteq X\setminus Y$ but not $e_{12}(1)$,
so the group is not simple.

\subsection*{An example}

\section{LEF groups}\label{sec:lef}

Kionke and Schesler asked which groups embed in finitely generated simple
amenable groups~\cite{KionkeSchesler}. Corollary~\ref{cor:lef} answers the
analogous question for simple Kazhdan groups that are limits of finite
simple groups. Its second statement is the analogue for LEF groups of the
theorem of Boone and Higman that a finitely generated group has solvable
word problem if and only if it embeds in a simple subgroup of a finitely
presented group~\cite{BooneHigman}, in Thompson's form, where the simple
group is finitely generated and has solvable word problem~\cite{Thompson80}.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:lef}
A finitely generated group is LEF if and only if it is a subgroup of an
infinite, finitely generated, simple Kazhdan group that is an expander
limit of finite simple groups $\SL_N(\F_2)$. For every finitely generated
LEF group $\Gamma$ there is such a group containing $\Gamma$ whose word
problem has the Turing degree of the word problem of $\Gamma$. So $\Gamma$
has solvable word problem if and only if it is a subgroup of such a group
with solvable word problem.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:host}
Every countable LEF group is a subgroup of such a group. The members of a
set of finitely generated groups embed in a single such group if and only
if they are LEF and represent countably many isomorphism classes. So one
such group contains every recursively presented finitely generated LEF
group, for instance every finitely presented residually finite group and
every $G_Y$ with $L(Y)$ recursive (Corollary~\ref{cor:wp}).
\end{corollary}

Simple hosts with only one of property~\textup{(T)} and LEF were known.
Without property~\textup{(T)}, Kionke and Schesler proved that every
finitely generated residually finite group embeds in a finitely generated
simple LEF group~\cite[Theorem~1.2]{KionkeSchesler}, so with Wilson's
theorem~\cite{Wilson} one simple LEF group contains every finitely
presented residually finite group. Without LEF, every countable group
embeds in a finitely generated simple group~\cite{Gorjuskin,Schupp}, and
even in a simple quotient of a torsion-free hyperbolic Kazhdan
group~\cite[Theorem~1.5]{CoulonFournierFacio}, so one simple Kazhdan group
contains Higman's universal finitely presented group~\cite{Higman}.

We embed a countable LEF group in the derived subgroup of a $2$-generated
LEF group $\Delta$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:nn}), and then $[\Delta,\Delta]$ in a
group of Theorem~\ref{thm:general}\textup{(b)} built from a lamplighter
action of $\Delta$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:host}). Lemma~\ref{lem:halfline} keeps
the Turing degree of the word problem in both steps.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:nn}
Every countable LEF group is a subgroup of $[\Delta,\Delta]$ for an
infinite LEF group $\Delta$ generated by two elements.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $\Gamma=\{\gamma_1,\gamma_2,\dots\}$ be a countable LEF group;
replacing $\Gamma$ by $\Gamma\times\Z$, we may assume that $\Gamma$ is
infinite. There are finite groups $Q_k$ and maps $\mu_k\colon\Gamma\to Q_k$
that are injective on $\{1,\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_k\}$ and preserve the
products that stay in this set. On $Q_k\times\Z/m_k$, with $m_k=|Q_k|$, let
$t_k$ add $1$ to the second coordinate, and let $f_{jk}$ multiply the first
coordinate of $(x,i)$ from the left by $\mu_k(\gamma_j)^i$, which is well
defined because the order of $\mu_k(\gamma_j)$ divides $m_k$. Then
$[f_{jk},t_k]$ multiplies the first coordinate by $\mu_k(\gamma_j)$, as in
the embedding of B.~H.~Neumann and H.~Neumann~\cite{NeumannNeumann}. On
$Q_k\times\Z/m_k\times\Z/4^{k+1}$ let $b_k$ add $1$ to the last
coordinate, and let $a_k$ act on the level $i$, the set of points with last
coordinate $i$, by $f_{jk}$ if $i=2^{2j-1}$ and by $t_k$ if $i=4^j$, for
$j\le k$, and trivially on the other levels. Then $b_k^{-n}a_kb_k^n$ acts
on the level $i$ as $a_k$ acts on the level $i+n$. As distinct ordered
pairs of distinct powers of $2$ have distinct nonzero differences, all of
absolute value below a quarter of the modulus, the two conjugates in
$w_j=[b^{-2^{2j-1}}ab^{2^{2j-1}},\,b^{-4^j}ab^{4^j}]$, evaluated at
$(a_k,b_k)$, cannot both act nontrivially on a level other than $0$. So
$w_j(a_k,b_k)$ acts by $[f_{jk},t_k]$ on the level $0$ and trivially on
the other levels, for $j\le k$. In the algebraic ultraproduct over $\omega$
of the symmetric groups of these sets, sending $\gamma$ to the class of the
permutations multiplying the first coordinate on the level $0$ by
$\mu_k(\gamma)$ and acting trivially elsewhere is a homomorphism,
injective as $\mu_k(\gamma)\ne1$ for $\gamma\ne1$ and large $k$. It sends
$\gamma_j$ to $w_j(a,b)$, where $a=(a_k)_k$ and $b=(b_k)_k$, so
$\Gamma\le[\Delta,\Delta]$ for the group $\Delta$ generated by $a$ and $b$.
Finitely many equations and inequalities that hold in the ultraproduct
hold in some coordinate, so $\Delta$ is LEF, and $\Delta$ is infinite
because it contains $\Gamma$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:host}
For a finitely generated group $\Delta$, let $\Delta$ act on
$\Omega=\F_2^{\Delta}$ by $(\delta x)(\eta)=x(\delta^{-1}\eta)$, let
$\Lambda$ be the group of maps $x\mapsto\delta x+c$ with $\delta\in\Delta$
and $c$ finitely supported, and put
$R_\Delta=\LC(\Omega,\F_2)\rtimes\Lambda$ and $G_\Delta=\EL_3(R_\Delta)$.
If $\Delta$ is infinite and LEF, then $G_\Delta$ is an infinite, finitely
generated, simple Kazhdan group and an expander limit of finite simple
groups $\SL_{3N}(\F_2)$, and $\xi\mapsto\operatorname{diag}(u_\xi,1,1)$
embeds $[\Lambda,\Lambda]$, which contains $[\Delta,\Delta]$, in
$G_\Delta$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The group $\Lambda$ is generated by $\Delta$ and the map $z$ adding $1$ at
$e$, and its orbits contain the dense sets $x+\bigoplus_\Delta\F_2$. If
$(\delta,c)\ne(e,0)$, the fixed points of $x\mapsto\delta x+c$ satisfy
$x(\eta)=x(\delta^{-1}\eta)+c(\eta)$ for all $\eta$. In every cylinder over
a finite set $W$ this fails at some point, for
$\eta\in\operatorname{supp}c$ if $\delta=e$ and for
$\eta\notin W\cup\delta W\cup\operatorname{supp}c$ if $\delta\ne e$. So the
action is minimal and topologically free. The ring $R_\Delta$ is generated
by $1$, the units $u_\xi^{\pm1}$ for $\xi$ in a finite generating set of
$\Lambda$, and the indicator $e_U$ of $U=\{x:x(e)=1\}$.

Let $\sigma_n\colon\Delta\to Q'_n$ be finite models of $\Delta$ that are
injective on the ball of radius $n$ and preserve the products that stay in
it, with $\sigma_n(e)=e$ and $Q'_n$ generated by the images of the
generators. To $\xi\colon x\mapsto\delta x+c$ in $\Lambda$ assign the
permutation $p_\xi$ of $Y_n=\F_2^{Q'_n}$ given by
$y\mapsto\sigma_n(\delta)y+\sum_{\eta\in\operatorname{supp}c}1_{\sigma_n(\eta)}$,
where $(qy)(q')=y(q^{-1}q')$, and attach to $y$ the point $y\circ\sigma_n$
of $\Omega$. For fixed $\xi,\eta\in\Lambda$ and large $n$ we have
$p_\xi p_\eta=p_{\xi\eta}$, and for every finite $W\subseteq\Delta$ and
large $n$ the attached points satisfy
$(p_\xi y)\circ\sigma_n=\xi(y\circ\sigma_n)$ on $W$ for all $y$, since
$\sigma_n$ respects the finitely many products and inequalities involved.
Let $D_n(f)$ be the diagonal matrix on $\F_2^{Y_n}$ with entry
$f(y\circ\sigma_n)$ at $y$, let $P_\xi$ be the permutation matrix of
$p_\xi$, and put
$\varphi_n(\sum_\xi f_\xi u_\xi)=\sum_\xi D_n(f_\xi)P_\xi$. For fixed $f$
and $\xi$ and large $n$,
\[
  P_\xi D_n(f)P_\xi^{-1}=D_n(f\circ\xi^{-1}),
\]
so for fixed $r,r'\in R_\Delta$ and large $n$, $\varphi_n$ is additive and
multiplicative on $r,r'$, and $\varphi_n(1)=I$. The conjugates of
$D_n(e_U)$ by products of the $P_\xi$ with $\xi$ in the generating set are
the coordinate functions of $Y_n$,
and the conjugates of $P_z$ are the translations by the $1_q$, so the
images of the generators generate $M_{2^{|Q'_n|}}(\F_2)$. Therefore
Theorem~\ref{thm:general} applies to $R_\Delta$.

Finally, $\xi\mapsto\operatorname{diag}(u_\xi,1,1)$ is an injective
homomorphism $\Lambda\to\GL_3(R_\Delta)$. It maps $[\Lambda,\Lambda]$ into
$G_\Delta$, since over $\F_2$ every unit $v$ satisfies
$\operatorname{diag}(v,v^{-1},1)=e_{12}(v)e_{21}(v^{-1})e_{12}(v)\,e_{12}(1)e_{21}(1)e_{12}(1)$,
and the product of these matrices for $v=v_1$, $v_2$ and $(v_2v_1)^{-1}$ is
$\operatorname{diag}(v_1v_2v_1^{-1}v_2^{-1},1,1)$. As $\Delta\le\Lambda$,
the group $[\Lambda,\Lambda]$ contains $[\Delta,\Delta]$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:host} and of the first statement
of Corollary~\ref{cor:lef}]
Limits of finite groups in the space of marked groups are LEF, and so are
their subgroups. Conversely, by Lemmas~\ref{lem:nn} and~\ref{lem:host}
every countable LEF group is a subgroup of a group $G_\Delta$ as in the
corollaries. For the second statement of Corollary~\ref{cor:host}, apply
the first to the restricted direct sum of representatives of countably many
isomorphism classes of finitely generated LEF groups, which is a countable
LEF group, as finite subsets lie in finite products. Conversely, a countable
group has countably many finitely generated subgroups. The last statement
of Corollary~\ref{cor:host} follows, as there are countably many recursive
presentations.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Solvable word problem}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:halfline}
\textup{(a)} Every finitely generated LEF group $\Gamma$ is a subgroup of
$[\Delta,\Delta]$ for an infinite finitely generated LEF group $\Delta$
whose word problem is Turing reducible to that of $\Gamma$.
\textup{(b)} The word problem of $G_\Delta$ is Turing reducible to that of
$\Delta$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $\Gamma$ be a finitely generated LEF group, generated by a finite set
$E$. We use a half-line form of the Neumann--Neumann construction, which
Darbinyan used to preserve solvable word problems~\cite{Darbinyan15}. On
$\Gamma\times\Z$, whose second coordinate is the level, let $\beta$ raise
the level by $1$, and let $h_\gamma$ multiply the first coordinate from the
left by $\gamma$ on the levels $m\ge0$. Then $[\beta,h_\gamma]$ multiplies
the level $0$ by $\gamma^{-1}$, so
$\Delta=\langle\beta,h_\gamma:\gamma\in E\rangle$ is infinite and
$\Gamma\le[\Delta,\Delta]$. Let $\lambda\ge1$. An element of $\Delta$ of
length at most $\lambda$ is $f\beta^\ell$ with $|\ell|\le\lambda$, where $f$ multiplies the
level $m$ from the left by an element $f(m)$ of length at most $\lambda$,
with $f(m)=1$ for $m<-\lambda$ and $f(m)=f(\lambda)$ for $m>\lambda$. Let
$\psi$ map the ball of radius $2\lambda$ of $\Gamma$ injectively into a
finite group $Q$, preserving the products that stay in it, and let $\theta$
be the isomorphism from $\{v\in Q^{[-3\lambda,3\lambda]}:v_{3\lambda-1}=v_{3\lambda}\}$
onto $\{v\in Q^{[-3\lambda,3\lambda]}:v_{-3\lambda}=1\}$ given by
$\theta(v)_{-3\lambda}=1$ and $\theta(v)_m=v_{m-1}$ for $m>-3\lambda$. The
bijection $\kappa$ of $Q^{[-3\lambda,3\lambda]}$ given by
$\kappa(x)_{-3\lambda}=x_{3\lambda-1}^{-1}x_{3\lambda}$ and
$\kappa(x)_m=x_{m-1}$ for $m>-3\lambda$ satisfies
$\kappa(vx)=\theta(v)\kappa(x)$ for $v$ in the domain of $\theta$, as
$v_{3\lambda-1}=v_{3\lambda}$ cancels in the coordinate $-3\lambda$. Let
each $v\in Q^{[-3\lambda,3\lambda]}$ act on
$Q^{[-3\lambda,3\lambda]}\times\Z/5\lambda$ by left multiplication on the
first coordinate, and put $\zeta(x,i)=(\kappa(x),i+1)$, so that
$\zeta v\zeta^{-1}=\theta(v)$ on the domain of $\theta$. As $f$ is trivial
below $-\lambda$ and constant from $\lambda$ on,
$\zeta^\ell(\psi(f(m)))_m\zeta^{-\ell}=(\psi(f(m-\ell)))_m$ for
$|\ell|\le\lambda$. So $f\beta^\ell\mapsto(\psi(f(m)))_{|m|\le3\lambda}\,\zeta^\ell$
preserves the products that stay in the ball of radius $\lambda$, and it is
injective there, as $5\lambda>2\lambda$ and $f$ is determined by its values
for $|m|\le3\lambda$. Therefore $\Delta$ is LEF.

From a word in the $\beta^{\pm1}$ and $h_\gamma^{\pm1}$ one computes the
exponent sum $\ell$ of $\beta$, the levels where $f$ changes, namely the
exponent sums of $\beta$ before the letters $h_\gamma^{\pm1}$, and the
values of $f$ there as words in $E$. It is trivial if and only if $\ell=0$
and these values are trivial in $\Gamma$, so the word problem of $\Delta$
is Turing reducible to that of $\Gamma$. This proves~(a).

For~(b), an element $x\mapsto\delta x+c$ of $\Lambda$ is given by a word
for $\delta$ and words for the points of $\operatorname{supp}c$, so an
oracle for the word problem of $\Delta$ decides equality in $\Lambda$
and computes the values of $c$. A word in the generators of $G_\Delta$
multiplies out to a matrix with entries $\sum_\xi p_\xi u_\xi$, where each
$p_\xi$ is a polynomial over $\F_2$ in finitely many coordinates
$x(\delta)$, as $u_\xi e_Uu_\xi^{-1}$ is the indicator of
$\{x:x(\delta)=1+c(\delta)\}$ for $\xi\colon x\mapsto\delta x+c$. After
collecting equal $\xi$ and equal coordinates, the word is trivial if and
only if every coefficient of its difference from $I_3$ vanishes at every
assignment of its finitely many variables, as $\Omega$ is the full shift.
So the word problem of $G_\Delta$ is Turing reducible to that of $\Delta$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of the second statement of Corollary~\ref{cor:lef}]
Let $\Gamma$ be a finitely generated LEF group. By
Lemmas~\ref{lem:halfline} and~\ref{lem:host}, $\Gamma$ is a subgroup of a
group $G_\Delta$ as in Corollary~\ref{cor:lef} whose word problem is Turing
reducible to that of $\Gamma$. Conversely, a word in the generators of
$\Gamma$ becomes a word in the generators of $G_\Delta$ by substitution,
and it is trivial in $\Gamma$ if and only if its image is trivial in
$G_\Delta$, so the word problem of $\Gamma$ reduces to that of $G_\Delta$.
The last statement follows, as finitely generated subgroups of groups with
solvable word problem have solvable word problem and LEF passes to
subgroups.
\end{proof}

\section{Word problems}\label{sec:wp}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:wp}
The word problem of $G_X$ has the Turing degree of the language $L(X)$ of
$X$, the set of words occurring in points of $X$, so $G_X$ has solvable
word problem if and only if $L(X)$ is recursive, as for the Fibonacci
subshift $X_{(3-\sqrt5)/2}$ below. Every Turing degree is the degree of the
word problem of some $G_X$, and there is a family of continuum many groups
$G_X$, none of which embeds in another.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Multiplying out a word in the generators over $\LC(A^{\Z},\F_2)\rtimes\Z$,
which maps onto $R$, with the rule $uf=(f\circ T^{-1})u$ gives a matrix
with entries $\sum_jf_ju^j$, where each $f_j$ is given by a table on the
words of some length. The word is trivial in $G_X$ if and only if the
tables of its difference from $I_3$ vanish on $L(X)$, so $L(X)$ computes
the word problem. Conversely, by~\eqref{eq:elementary} a word for
$e_{12}(\prod_{t<m}u^{-t}e_{v_t}u^t)$ is computable from
$v=v_0\cdots v_{m-1}$, and it is trivial if and only if $v\notin L(X)$.

For irrational $\alpha\in(0,1)$ let $X_\alpha$ be the infinite minimal
Sturmian subshift, the closure of the codings $c(\theta)$,
$\theta\in[0,1)$, with
$c(\theta)_t=\lfloor\theta+(t+1)\alpha\rfloor-\lfloor\theta+t\alpha\rfloor$,
which is $1$ exactly for $\theta$ in the arc $[-(t+1)\alpha,-t\alpha)$
modulo $1$~\cite{MorseHedlund,Hedlund44},~\cite[Chapter~2]{Lothaire}.
Passing to the closure adds no words, so the words of length $m$ of
$X_\alpha$ are the constant values of $c(\theta)_{[0,m)}$ on the arcs
between the distinct points $-j\alpha\bmod1$, $0\le j\le m$, and $\alpha$
computes $L(X_\alpha)$. Such a word has $\lfloor\theta+m\alpha\rfloor$
ones, within $1$ of $m\alpha$, so $L(X_\alpha)$ computes $\alpha$. Every
$D\subseteq\mathbb N$ has the degree of
$[0;1+\chi_D(0),1+\chi_D(1),\dots]$, since each digit is decided by one
strict comparison with a rational computed from earlier digits.
If a finitely generated group $H$ embeds in a finitely generated group
$G$, a word for $H$ becomes a word for $G$ by substituting words in the
generators of $G$ for the generators of $H$, so the word problem of $H$
reduces to that of $G$. By a construction of Sacks, the Turing degrees
contain an antichain of size continuum~\cite[Chapter~V]{Odifreddi}, and the
groups $G_{X_\alpha}$ with $\alpha$ of these degrees are as claimed.
\end{proof}

For derived topological full groups, Grigorchuk and Medynets proved that
the word problem is solvable if and only if $L(X)$ is
recursive~\cite[Theorem~1.1(3)]{GMpres}.

\section{Questions}\label{sec:questions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item\label{q:fp} Is there an infinite finitely presented simple group
with property~\textup{(T)} that is sofic, or at least hyperlinear?
Infinite finitely presented simple Kazhdan groups
exist~\cite{CapraceRemy}, while no group in
Theorem~\ref{thm:general}\textup{(b)} is finitely presented: a finitely
presented LEF group is residually finite~\cite{Stepin,VershikGordon}, and
an infinite simple group is not. A sofic example would not be LEF, and it
would also answer
Open problem~6.1 of Alekseev and Thom~\cite{AlekseevThom}, which asks for
finitely presented sofic groups with property~\textup{(T)} that are not
residually finite.
\item Is every sofic infinite simple Kazhdan group LEF? A sofic example
for Question~\ref{q:fp} would answer this negatively.
\item Is every finitely generated sofic group a subgroup of an infinite,
finitely generated, simple Kazhdan sofic group? Corollary~\ref{cor:lef}
gives this for LEF groups, with LEF hosts.
\item If $(X,T)$ is flip conjugate to $(Y,T')$, that is, topologically
conjugate to $(Y,T')$ or to $(Y,T'^{-1})$, then $G_X\cong G_Y$, and for
topological full groups the converse holds~\cite{GPS99,BezuglyiMedynets}.
Does $G_X\cong G_Y$ imply that $X$ and $Y$ are flip conjugate? In a sequel
we show that it implies that $X$ and $Y$ are strongly orbit
equivalent~\cite{GPS}. Does $L(G_X)\cong L(G_Y)$ imply $G_X\cong G_Y$?
\end{enumerate}
